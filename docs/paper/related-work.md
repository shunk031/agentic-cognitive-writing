# Related work

Prior work frames four parts of the research problem: a cognitive account of composing, staged systems for long-form generation, human-AI writing-support tools, and methods for evaluating writing quality. We connect the process-oriented strands through an executable, inspectable process while keeping product evaluation separate from process analysis.

## Cognitive process theories of writing

Flower and Hayes' Cognitive Process Theory of Writing[^1] replaces a stage account of composing with a model of interacting processes. The theory distinguishes Planning, Translating, and Reviewing under the control of a Monitor, and it places those processes in interaction with the task environment and the writer's long-term memory. Planning includes generating ideas, organizing them, and setting goals; Translating turns represented meanings into written language; and Reviewing evaluates and revises both text and plans. The processes can be embedded and re-entered as needed, so revision is not restricted to an end stage.

The theory explains coherence through a hierarchical network of goals that develops during composing. The network links broad rhetorical aims to more operational sub-goals and supports returns to higher-level goals when local work reveals new constraints. The theory also allows writers to regenerate goals when writing changes what they understand about the task. Flower and Hayes ground these claims in protocol analysis of composing activity rather than in a fixed sequence of completed products.

Bereiter and Scardamalia's knowledge-telling versus knowledge-transforming distinction[^2] adds a developmental account of how writers can relate retrieved content to a rhetorical problem. Knowledge-telling retrieves content associated with a topic and converts it into prose with limited transformation of the rhetorical problem, whereas knowledge-transforming coordinates content knowledge with the demands of that problem. For this paper, the distinction motivates using goal-regeneration events in the process trace as an observable process measure of whether the system revises its represented rhetorical problem, without treating those events as direct evidence of private cognition.

## Large language model long-form writing systems

STORM[^3] is a long-form Wikipedia-like writing system organized as a research-to-draft pipeline. The system discovers perspectives, asks simulated perspective-specific questions, and curates information. It then builds an outline, generates an article, and polishes the result. The released STORM implementation[^18] separates these stages into modules. The paper reports results on FreshWiki plus feedback from experienced Wikipedia editors. Those results characterize STORM's retrieval-grounded setup, so they do not establish the behavior of a retrieval-free comparator.

The STORM-style baseline is explicitly retrieval-free under the equal-information policy. The baseline receives only the supplied assignment and context, runs perspective discovery, simulated question answering, outlining, drafting, and polishing, and omits citation generation. Claims about STORM's retrieval-grounded results do not transfer to this baseline. The experiment uses the baseline to compare stage organization under common information.

CogWriter[^21] uses cognitive writing theory to guide constrained long-form generation. The paper cites the 1981 cognitive process theory and presents hierarchical planning, continuous monitoring, and dynamic reviewing as cognitive writing mechanisms. The released implementation[^22] executes planning before generation. It contains separate Planning Agent and Generation Agent modules, but no Monitor module or explicit goal network. Its checks revise the plan, enforce task constraints, and adjust output length within that fixed top-level order. The reported evaluation is confined to LongGenBench. It includes no trace analysis. The released artifact is a command-line research framework rather than an installable writing plugin. CogWriter thus represents theory-inspired performance engineering. Our design instead operationalizes the theory through persistent artifacts, Monitor-mediated dynamic process selection, and append-only traces. The controlled comparison tests the theory's predictions.

### Adjacent research and editor-integrated systems

PaperDebugger[^4] is described as an in-editor, plugin-based multi-agent assistant for academic writing, review, and editing. Its released implementation[^19] includes a Claude skill and a chat-streaming API, which anchor the implementation description.

PaperQA[^5] is a retrieval-augmented research agent for scientific-literature question answering, with search and index modules documented in its released implementation[^20].

For the surveyed set that includes these adjacent systems, the supporting system survey (see Artifact availability) does not identify a system that operationalizes the theory as persistent artifacts with an explicit goal network, uses Monitor-mediated dynamic process selection, records append-only traces, packages these mechanisms as an installable plugin, and tests Flower and Hayes' predictions.[^1]

The comparison concerns process control rather than the presence of multiple agents. For systems that implement research or planning, drafting, and polishing as named stages, the released STORM implementation[^3] provides a concrete pipeline reference. The linear Pre-Write/Write/Re-Write baseline and the STORM-style baseline without retrieval make fixed stage organization explicit under the common-information policy. The experiment compares those baselines with a recursive Monitor that records process switches and goal changes.

## Human and artificial intelligence (AI) co-writing and writing-support tools

CoAuthor[^6] establishes a process-oriented dataset for human-AI collaborative writing. The dataset records writer prompts, model continuations, acceptance behavior, and revisions, making interaction visible beyond the final document. CoAuthor is a precedent for studying collaboration traces, although its central object is the interaction record rather than a theory-derived controller for the writing process.

The In2Writing design-space work uses cognitive process theory to organize writing-support research. Gero et al.'s design-space paper[^7] defines a design space around the part of writing supported by a tool and the constraints on the supported writing goal, drawing on the non-linear, goal-directed model of Flower and Hayes[^1]. Their review of writing-support work identifies gaps in which process areas and goal constraints have received attention. The design-space paper uses the theory as a taxonomy for comparing tools, whereas our plugin uses the theory to specify executable roles, transitions, goal files, and trace events.

The wider In2Writing series supplies complementary process precedents. Schneider et al.'s paper[^8] relates data-to-text pipeline architecture to research on human writing processes and argues that process-level optimization matters when evaluating every generated output is impractical. Du et al.'s Read, Revise, Repeat system[^9] lets a model propose edits, lets writers accept or reject them, and feeds accepted edits into later iterations. Liu and August's paper[^10] grounds an intelligent writing tool in writing-center tutoring practices, while Kim et al.'s paper[^11] proposes a formative study comparing spoken and written interaction with conversational agents.

## Writing-quality evaluation

The evaluation design combines broad, long-text, and structured writing benchmarks. WritingBench[^12] supplies general-purpose writing queries with instance-specific criteria, HelloBench[^13] targets long-text generation across several task types, and DoLoMiTes[^14] supplies methodical expert-authored tasks suited to reports and design documents. The evaluation-dataset survey selects these three as the primary set. The survey treats PERSUADE 2.0[^16] as an argumentative prompt and human-quality anchor and ICLE++[^17] as an external persuasive-essay trait anchor for rubric calibration, rather than as direct substitutes for model-quality labels.

Judge methodology must account for both rubric variation and pairwise presentation effects. The evaluation protocol specifies a Prometheus-2-style open evaluator[^15], assigns two judge families, uses a five-dimension pointwise rubric with evidence quotes, and evaluates every condition pair in both A/B orders with blinded labels. Human validation samples pairwise judgments with three annotators per comparison. The protocol freezes these settings before scoring and reports product quality separately from trace-derived measures.

Process evaluation complements final-output evaluation in this setting. The protocol analyzes goal quantity and specificity, middle-range goal quantity and quality, goal regeneration, process-switch transitions, interruptions, pop-back events, and revision intent, and relates those measures to per-prompt quality. The trace is an operational analogue of a thinking-aloud protocol, not a direct transcript of private reasoning, so missing or ambiguous events remain data-quality findings rather than inferred cognition.

## Positioning

Our positioning uses an executable comparison criterion rather than a claim about agent count. A system meets the criterion only if it operationalizes Flower and Hayes' theory[^1] as persistent artifacts with an explicit goal network, uses Monitor-mediated dynamic process selection, records append-only traces, packages these mechanisms as an installable plugin, and tests the theory's predictions. To our knowledge, the supporting system survey identifies no surveyed system that meets this criterion. The present work implements the mapping and specifies a controlled comparison.
