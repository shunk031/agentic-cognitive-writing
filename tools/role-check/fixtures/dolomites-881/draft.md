# Environmental Impact Assessment: Improved Irrigation Technologies in the Indus Basin

## Executive Summary

The project will replace traditional surface irrigation in the Indus Basin of Pakistan with drip and sprinkler systems powered by subsidized electricity, solar energy, or diesel engines, to raise agricultural productivity and slow groundwater depletion. The assessment finds that the main environmental pressures at the site are severe groundwater depletion from excessive abstraction, high CO2 emissions from diesel pumping, low water use efficiency, and soil and water degradation from the overuse of fertilizers and pesticides. The proposed mitigation measures are the switch to efficient irrigation to cut water losses, a preference for solar power over diesel to reduce emissions, and monitoring of air, water, soil, and biodiversity against the national standards throughout implementation, with local communities and regulators engaged from the planning stage onward.

## Legal and Regulatory Compliance

The project complies with the Pakistan Environmental Protection Act (1997) and the National Environmental Quality Standards (NEQS), which govern air and water quality, waste management, and biodiversity conservation. Implementation follows the guidelines of the Punjab Irrigated-Agriculture Productivity Improvement Project (PIPIP) for installing improved irrigation technologies in the region, which aligns the project with recognized good practice. The permits and licenses obtained for the project set specific conditions for environmental protection, and the project will meet those conditions as part of its commitment to the applicable environmental standards.

## Baseline Environmental Conditions

The Indus Basin faces severe groundwater depletion because of excessive abstraction for irrigation, and traditional irrigation methods lose a significant share of the water they draw. Energy use for pumping, mainly by diesel engines, makes CO2 emissions in the region high. Soil and water quality are already affected by the overuse of fertilizers and pesticides, so the project starts from degraded rather than pristine conditions.

Monitoring data show that air quality meets the standards, with concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, CO, and O3 within acceptable limits, and meteorological records describe the local wind patterns and temperature variations. Water quality is tracked through pH, dissolved oxygen, biochemical and chemical oxygen demand, turbidity, and pollutant concentrations. Biodiversity assessments record the diversity of flora and fauna, habitat maps, and species-specific data, which together describe the ecological interactions that the project must protect.
