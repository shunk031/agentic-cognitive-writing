\begin{abstract}
Long-form writing systems structure generation through plans, sections, subtasks, and revision rounds, but this leaves open what a writing controller should choose next. We test a different abstraction drawn from the cognitive process theory of writing~\cite{flower1981cognitive}: a \texttt{Monitor} chooses among \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} over a shared draft, goal representation, and process history. Across three complementary long-form writing benchmarks and independent replications, this process-level writer is preferred to single-pass, staged, and adaptive task-planning writers. The mechanism results are less supportive of elaborate control: removing the explicit goal network or fixing the process order produces no detectable loss under our confirmatory test, and the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows the canonical process cycle while rarely regenerating goals. Folding the process roles into one writer produces a large loss, but that intervention also changes recursion granularity and output length, so it does not isolate delegation. Compute-matched analyses preserve the advantage over staged and task-planning writers, while a cross-family judge preserves pooled directions but commits on only a minority of pairs. These results suggest that cognitive writing theory may be more useful as a decomposition of writing computation than as a literal dynamic scheduling policy for current language agents.
\end{abstract}
