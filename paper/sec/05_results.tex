\subsection{Direct Comparison of Writing Systems}
\label{sec:results-effectiveness}

Direct same-prompt comparison produces a clearer system-level separation. Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} shows that \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to \condSinglePass, \condStaged, and \condTaskPlanning, with across-benchmark mean win rates of \PooledFourOneRateMean, \PooledFourTwoRateMean, and \PooledFourThreeRateMean, respectively, among comparisons where both presentation orders agree on a winner. The direction holds in every benchmark and generation run against \condSinglePass\ and \condTaskPlanning, and in all but one benchmark--run test against \condStaged; Appendix~\ref{app:pairwise-detail} reports the run-level intervals and multiple-comparison-corrected decisions. Counting every attempted prompt instead of conditioning on an agreed winner yields win shares of \UncondFullSinglePassWinShare, \UncondFullStagedWinShare, and \UncondFullTaskPlanningWinShare. Even assigning every missing pair against \condAgenticCogWriter\ leaves decided-comparison win rates of at least \WorstCaseFullSinglePassRate, \WorstCaseFullStagedRate, and \WorstCaseFullTaskPlanningRate.

\input{tab/pairwise-results}

The direct-comparison result supports the tested system as a whole rather than a single mechanism. \condAgenticCogWriter\ differs from the alternatives in several coupled design choices, including persistent writing state, a process-level action space, and repeated return to the \texttt{Monitor}. \subsection{Ablations and Execution Diagnostics}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

Neither pre-specified ablation identifies a large standalone contribution from explicit goals or adaptive process order. Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} shows that no benchmark--run test for \condAgenticCogWriter\ versus \condNoGoals\ or \condFixedOrder\ remains significant after the within-benchmark Holm correction. In the exploratory prompt-level summary, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to \condFixedOrder\ at \ClusteredFullFixedOrderRate\ [\ClusteredFullFixedOrderInterval], $p=\ClusteredFullFixedOrderP$, while its preference over \condNoGoals\ is \ClusteredFullNoGoalsRate\ [\ClusteredFullNoGoalsInterval], $p=\ClusteredFullNoGoalsP$. The first estimate suggests a small benefit from adaptive ordering; the second does not provide evidence for a comparable effect from the explicit goal representation. Because the summary was defined after the main results, both estimates remain exploratory.

Execution changes produce a larger but less easily isolated difference. \condSingleWriter\ removes subagent delegation, changes role context and decision granularity, and alters output length at the same time. \subsection{How Does the Writing Loop Behave in Practice?}
\label{sec:results-process-dynamics}

The adaptive writing loop usually follows a simple process path. Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} shows that one \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle dominates the traces; returning to planning after review and replacing an existing goal are both rare. The system therefore has the ability to revisit earlier processes but uses that flexibility infrequently on the evaluated prompts. The behavior is consistent with the small exploratory advantage over \condFixedOrder: adaptive ordering can help only on runs where the controller has reason to depart from the default cycle.

\input{tab/process-dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} shows the same concentration at the sequence and transition levels. The most frequent complete process path is the forward planning--drafting--reviewing progression, and the unrestricted system is visually close to \condFixedOrder\ because much of its observed behavior follows that path. The demanding-subset selection increases input length, requested output length, or procedural burden, but it does not guarantee that every task intrinsically requires replanning. The traces therefore show that adaptive control is rarely exercised on these subsets, not that repeated replanning is unnecessary for difficult writing in general.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

The architecture-level direction persists under the tested length and compute restrictions where eligible pairs exist. Appendix~\ref{app:length-control} shows that \condAgenticCogWriter\ remains favored over \condSinglePass\ within the pre-specified output-length bands, despite an overall tendency for the primary evaluator to prefer longer outputs. Appendix~\ref{app:compute-control} shows the same favorable direction against \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning\ under the tightest available compute match; no \condAgenticCogWriter--\condSinglePass\ pairs satisfy that band. Because length and realized compute are consequences of execution, these analyses are descriptive sensitivity checks.

Judge identity produces more variation than the resource-matched checks. Appendix~\ref{app:cross-family} preserves the overall direction for the selected contrasts under the cross-family evaluator but yields many ties and reverses the WritingBench direction for \condAgenticCogWriter\ versus \condSinglePass. We therefore treat the cross-family result as partial robustness evidence rather than proof that the exact win rates are evaluator-invariant.
