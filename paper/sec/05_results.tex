\subsection{Pointwise and Benchmark-Native Quality}
\label{sec:results-pointwise}

Table~\ref{tab:main-results} shows that \condAgenticCogWriter\ obtains the strongest native and pointwise scores on WritingBench and HelloBench, whereas \condStaged\ has the strongest DoLoMiTes pointwise score. The table displays the four architecture-level systems, but each pointwise dimension is standardized against outputs from all seven pre-specified conditions before the five z-scores are averaged. The pointwise values are therefore relative: zero is the across-condition mean for that benchmark, and negative entries such as the \condSinglePass\ WritingBench score indicate below-reference performance rather than negative absolute document quality. Taken together, the independent metrics show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ is competitive across all three benchmark types but is not uniformly best.

\subsection{Direct Comparison of Writing Architectures}
\label{sec:results-effectiveness}

Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} shows a clearer separation among writing architectures when the judge directly compares responses to the same prompt. Its headline rates are mean win rates among \emph{decided} comparisons, meaning that both presentation orders agree on a winner; ties and pairs with a failed output are excluded from that denominator. Under this convention, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to one-shot generation, the fixed-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning, with across-benchmark mean win rates of \PooledFourOneRateMean, \PooledFourTwoRateMean, and \PooledFourThreeRateMean, respectively. The direction holds in every benchmark--generation-run cell against \condSinglePass\ and \condTaskPlanning, and in all but one cell against \condStaged; generation-run-level exact tests with within-benchmark Holm correction support this pattern, with intervals and cell-level decisions in Appendix~\ref{app:pairwise-detail}. When every attempted prompt is counted instead, so ties and failed pairs remain in the denominator, the corresponding win shares are \UncondFullSinglePassWinShare, \UncondFullStagedWinShare, and \UncondFullTaskPlanningWinShare; assigning every missing pair against \condAgenticCogWriter\ still leaves decided-comparison win rates of at least \WorstCaseFullSinglePassRate, \WorstCaseFullStagedRate, and \WorstCaseFullTaskPlanningRate, respectively.

\input{tab/pairwise-results}

Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} establishes an architecture-level difference but does not isolate a mechanism. \condAgenticCogWriter\ differs from the three alternatives in several coupled choices, including its persistent state, process-level action space, and repeated return to the \texttt{Monitor}. We therefore interpret the direct-comparison result as evidence for the tested organization as a whole rather than for any one of those features.

\subsection{Ablations and Execution Diagnostics}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} shows that the pre-specified cellwise tests do not identify a large contribution from either explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive process order. For both \condNoGoals\ and \condFixedOrder, no benchmark--generation-run cell survives the within-benchmark Holm correction. A post-hoc prompt-clustered analysis gives a more sensitive but exploratory pooled view: after averaging each prompt's win, tie, and loss outcomes over the three generation runs and pooling benchmarks, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to \condFixedOrder\ at \ClusteredFullFixedOrderRate\ [\ClusteredFullFixedOrderInterval], $p=\ClusteredFullFixedOrderP$, whereas its preference over \condNoGoals\ is \ClusteredFullNoGoalsRate\ [\ClusteredFullNoGoalsInterval], $p=\ClusteredFullNoGoalsP$. Because this analysis was not pre-specified, it does not replace the confirmatory cellwise family; together, the estimates indicate a small contribution from adaptive ordering and no significant pooled contribution from the explicit goal network, with both effects bounded near parity and too small to account for the substantially larger advantages over the architecture baselines.

\input{tab/ablation-results}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} also shows a much larger loss for \condSingleWriter, but that comparison changes several execution properties at once. It removes subagent delegation, changes role context, changes how much text can be produced between decisions, and changes output length. Within the tighter and wider pre-specified output-length matching bands, \condSingleWriter\ still wins only \SingleWriterLengthFiveRate\ ($p=\SingleWriterLengthFiveP$) and \SingleWriterLengthTenRate\ ($p=\SingleWriterLengthTenP$), respectively, against \condAgenticCogWriter, so output length does not explain that gap. The narrower post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check retains the process loop and shows no significant pooled difference from \condAgenticCogWriter\ in its exploratory generation run (\SingleContextFullRate\ win rate for \condSingleContext, $p=\SingleContextFullP$, unadjusted). Because that run also differs in gateway entry point and transport, these results weaken a simple role-context explanation without isolating an alternative mechanism; Appendix~\ref{app:single-context} gives the benchmark-level results.

\subsection{How Does the Writing Loop Behave in Practice?}
\label{sec:results-process-dynamics}

Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} shows that the supposedly flexible writing loop is behaviorally conservative. The dominant behavior is one \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle; a new planning step after review is rare, and replacement of an existing goal is also rare. Thus, although \condAgenticCogWriter\ can revisit earlier processes, most runs use that freedom very little. This concentration is consistent with the small exploratory advantage over \condFixedOrder: a controller that rarely departs from the default cycle has limited opportunity to benefit from being allowed to deviate.

\input{tab/process-dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} shows the same concentration at the sequence and transition levels. The most frequent complete process path is the forward planning--drafting--reviewing progression, and the unrestricted system is visually close to \condFixedOrder\ because much of its observed behavior follows that path. This result comes from the deliberately demanding benchmark subsets rather than a random collection of routine prompts, but the length-and-demand selector does not guarantee that every task intrinsically requires replanning or repeated revision. The trace evidence therefore shows that adaptive control is rarely exercised on the evaluated subsets rather than that it is unnecessary for all difficult writing tasks.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:appendix-length-control} and Table~\ref{tab:appendix-compute-control} show that the architecture-level direction survives the tested output-length and compute restrictions where eligible pairs exist. The main judge favors longer outputs overall, but \condAgenticCogWriter\ remains favored over \condSinglePass\ within the pre-specified length-matching bands. Under the tightest available compute match, the direction also remains favorable against \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning; no \condAgenticCogWriter--\condSinglePass\ pairs satisfy that compute band. Because output length and realized compute are consequences of execution, these checks are descriptive rather than causal adjustments.

Table~\ref{tab:appendix-cross-family} shows more judge dependence than the resource-matched checks. The cross-family judge preserves the pooled direction for the checked contrasts but produces many ties and reverses the WritingBench direction for \condAgenticCogWriter\ versus \condSinglePass. We therefore interpret it as a partial robustness check rather than as confirmation that the exact win rates are judge-invariant.
