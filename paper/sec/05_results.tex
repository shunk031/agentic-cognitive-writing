\subsection{Direct comparison of writing architectures}
\label{sec:results-effectiveness}

Direct same-prompt comparisons give a clearer separation among the writing architectures than the independent scores. Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} reports \condAgenticCogWriter{}'s mean win rate across replications after requiring agreement across the two presentation orders for each prompt. The proposed architecture is preferred to one-shot generation, the fixed-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning, with across-benchmark mean win rates of \PooledFourOneRateMean, \PooledFourTwoRateMean, and \PooledFourThreeRateMean, respectively. The direction holds in every benchmark--replication cell against \condSinglePass\ and \condTaskPlanning, and in all but one cell against \condStaged. Replication-level exact tests with within-benchmark Holm correction support this pattern; Appendix~\ref{app:pairwise-detail} reports the confidence intervals and cell-level decisions.

\input{tab/pairwise-results}

The result is architectural rather than mechanistic. \condAgenticCogWriter\ differs from the three alternatives in several coupled choices, including its persistent state, process-level action space, and repeated return to the \texttt{Monitor}. The comparison therefore establishes a performance difference among the tested organizations but does not identify which individual feature causes it.

\subsection{Ablations and execution diagnostics}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

The ablations rule out two intuitive single-component explanations for the architecture-level advantage. Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} shows that removing the explicit goal network (\condNoGoals) leaves the pairwise comparison close to even, with no benchmark--replication cell surviving Holm correction. Fixing the process order to planning, drafting, and reviewing (\condFixedOrder) yields the same inferential pattern. We therefore do not find evidence that either explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive process ordering is individually necessary for the observed advantage. These non-significant differences do not establish equivalence between the ablations and the full system.

\input{tab/ablation-results}

Changing how the processes execute produces a larger but more difficult-to-interpret difference. \condSingleWriter\ performs substantially worse than \condAgenticCogWriter, but it simultaneously removes subagent delegation, changes role context, changes how much text can be produced between decisions, and changes output length. The narrower post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check retains the process loop and shows no significant pooled difference from \condAgenticCogWriter\ in its exploratory replication (\SingleContextFullRate\ win rate for \condSingleContext, $p=\SingleContextFullP$, unadjusted). Because that run also differs in gateway entry point and transport, these results weaken a simple role-context explanation without isolating an alternative mechanism; Appendix~\ref{app:single-context} gives the benchmark-level results.

\subsection{How does the writing loop behave in practice?}
\label{sec:results-process-dynamics}

The trace analysis reveals a surprisingly conservative writing policy: although \condAgenticCogWriter\ can revisit earlier processes, most runs use that freedom very little. Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} shows that the dominant behavior is one \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle. A new planning step after review is rare, and replacement of an existing goal is also rare. This pattern helps explain why imposing the same three-process order in \condFixedOrder\ produces little measurable degradation.

\input{tab/process-dynamics}

The dominance of a simple cycle is visible at both the sequence and transition levels. Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} shows the most frequent complete process paths and the transition counts for \condAgenticCogWriter\ and \condFixedOrder; the unrestricted system is visually close to the fixed-cycle variant because most of its probability mass lies on the same forward progression. Importantly, this behavior is observed on the pre-committed \texttt{hard100} slice, which deliberately favors long inputs and requested outputs, so it is not merely a result from a random collection of short prompts. At the same time, our length-based selection does not guarantee that a prompt requires replanning or repeated revision; the result therefore shows that adaptive control is rarely exercised on this demanding slice, not that it is unnecessary for every harder or more open-ended writing task.

\subsection{Robustness checks}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

The architecture-level direction is not explained away by the tested output-length and compute restrictions where eligible pairs exist. The main judge favors longer outputs overall, but \condAgenticCogWriter\ remains favored over \condSinglePass\ within the pre-specified length-matching bands. Under the tightest available compute match, the direction also remains favorable against \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning; no \condAgenticCogWriter--\condSinglePass\ pairs satisfy that compute band. Because output length and realized compute are consequences of execution, these checks are descriptive rather than causal adjustments.

Judge choice introduces more uncertainty than the resource checks. A cross-family judge preserves the pooled direction for the checked contrasts but produces many ties and reverses the WritingBench direction for \condAgenticCogWriter\ versus \condSinglePass. We therefore interpret it as a partial robustness check rather than as confirmation that the exact win rates are judge-invariant. Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity-analyses} reports the full length, compute, and cross-family results.
