We compare the writing systems under the same task inputs and information policy to study three questions: whether process-level organization improves document quality, which components contribute to any advantage, and how the writing loop behaves in practice.
Because agent execution is stochastic, every prompt--system pair is generated in three runs, so differences between systems can be judged against run-to-run variation.

\subsection{Systems Compared}
\label{sec:baselines}

We consider seven pre-specified systems under the same generation setup.
The numbering below groups them by role.

\paragraph{Alternative writing systems.}
We compare three alternatives to \condAgenticCogWriter: (1) \condSinglePass\ writes the complete document in one pass within one agent session; (2) \condStaged\ executes the \texttt{Pre-Write}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Write}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Re-Write} stage model~\citep{rohman1965pre}, with which \citet{flower1981cognitive} contrast their process model; and (3) \condTaskPlanning\ follows a WriteHERE-style adaptive task graph~\citep{xiong2025beyond}, recursively decomposing the assignment and selecting among dependency-satisfied task nodes.
The alternatives therefore advance a whole document, a fixed writing stage, or an adaptive task node, whereas our system advances a writing process.

\paragraph{Ablations of \condAgenticCogWriter.}
Two pre-specified ablations change one control mechanism while retaining the rest of the loop, though \condFixedOrder\ also omits our goal-logging check.
(4) \condNoGoals\ forbids the goals file and goal events but keeps adaptive process selection and the three subagents, whose \texttt{Planner} loads the shared planning skill but receives no goal-setting request, while (5) \condFixedOrder\ keeps the shared draft, goals, process history, and subagents but replaces the \texttt{Monitor}'s adaptive decision with a repeated \texttt{Planning}--\texttt{Translating}--\texttt{Reviewing} cycle.

\paragraph{Execution variant and full system.}
(6) \condSingleWriter\ retains the goal representation and adaptive process loop but performs \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} in one writer context without subagent delegation; it also limits each \texttt{Translating} step to one paragraph, so the condition is diagnostic rather than a clean ablation.
Its prompt also asks the writer to follow the repository's writing guidance and remove filler and AI-pattern phrasing, an instruction the other systems' prompts do not contain.
(7) \condAgenticCogWriter\ is the full system of Section~\ref{sec:method-writing-processes}, with adaptive process selection, explicit goals, and role-specific subagents.

\paragraph{Post-hoc conditions.}
Six conditions were added post hoc, outside the seven pre-specified systems.
\condStagedRoles\ gives \condStaged\ our role instructions; \condSingleContext\ runs each selected process in the \texttt{Monitor}'s context instead of a subagent; \condPlainAgent\ gives the same harness the task and one neutral instruction to plan, draft, and revise a workspace draft; \condExploratoryOne\ is our adaptation of CogWriter~\citep{wan2025cognitive}; \condSelfRefine\ follows Self-Refine~\citep{madaan2023self} with \SelfRefineRounds\ feedback rounds chosen on unscored prompts to approximate our token use, which it exceeded by \SelfRefineTokenExcess\ per completed document; and \condLoopOnly\ removes goals, adaptive ordering, and subagents together.

\subsection{Benchmarks and Prompts}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

We use three benchmarks to test whether process-level organization transfers across different forms of long-form writing: (1) WritingBench~\citep{wu2026writingbench}, which covers broad real-world writing requests with task-specific evaluation criteria; (2) HelloBench~\citep{que2024hellobench}, which includes long-text summarization, open-ended generation, and completion; and (3) DoLoMiTes~\citep{malaviya2025dolomites}, which targets methodical expert writing with explicit objectives, procedures, inputs, and constraints.
The benchmarks cover general instruction following, sustained long-context generation, and structured expert-document production.

We evaluate fixed demanding subsets because short or lightly constrained tasks may not reveal differences in how a system manages a long document.
We first reserve any prompts used while developing or debugging the evaluation protocol and never score those prompts.
From the remaining benchmark items, we deterministically prioritize cases with longer supplied context and, when the prompt specifies a target length, longer requested output, while applying fixed maximum-length limits.
This produces a different demanding subset for each benchmark: WritingBench emphasizes long inputs with explicit long-output requirements, HelloBench emphasizes very long-input summarization and continuation, and DoLoMiTes emphasizes procedurally constrained expert writing.\footnote{See Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} for the exact selection rule, sample sizes, and sensitivity analysis.}

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

All systems run in Codex CLI as the coding-agent harness with \modelGenBare\ as the generator; API-based scoring uses \modelJudgeBare\ as the primary evaluator.\footnote{We use Luna, which OpenAI positions for fast, cost-sensitive workloads, for high-volume long-form generation and the higher-capability tier, Sol, for evaluation; the shared model family motivates the cross-family check~\citep{openai2026gpt56,openai2026luna,openai2026sol}.}
The comparison holds the generator, harness, workspace tools, output budget, and task information constant across systems; \condStaged\ also runs as a Codex agent with file and shell tools, executing its stages in one session whose \texttt{Re-Write} stage rewrites the whole response.
Our system is an agent skill: its \texttt{Monitor} invokes \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, and \texttt{Reviewer} as subagents.\footnote{See Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} for the complete prompts and Appendix~\ref{app:runtime} for the runtime settings.}

Main-result tables summarize the three generation runs unless noted otherwise.
Network access, web search, and external retrieval are disabled for every system, and the harness records completed outputs plus available process traces, token accounting, and timing.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate documents with LLM-as-a-judge~\citep{zheng2023judging}, in which an evaluator model reads the prompt and the generated documents and returns rubric scores or a preference.
We use two protocols: pointwise evaluation scores each document alone, and pairwise evaluation compares two systems' documents per prompt.

\paragraph{Pointwise evaluation.}
When a benchmark defines its own rubric, we report that score as the \emph{benchmark-native} score, meaning the benchmark authors' original criteria rather than our shared rubric.
WritingBench uses five prompt-specific criteria scored from 1 to 10, which we divide by 10, HelloBench uses checklist items, and DoLoMiTes provides no comparable native score.

We also apply a common five-dimension rubric across all three benchmarks: instruction fulfillment, organization and coherence, content adequacy and depth, style and audience fit, and factual or constraint fidelity, each scored from 1 to 5.\footnote{See Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} for evaluation procedures and prompts.}
Because these ratings concentrate near the upper end, each dimension is standardized within each generation run and benchmark across outputs from all seven pre-specified systems before averaging.
The composite is relative within a benchmark rather than an absolute quality scale.

\paragraph{Pairwise evaluation.}
The judge uses the same quality dimensions and records a preference or a tie.
LLM judges can exhibit position bias~\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair}, so every pair is evaluated in both presentation orders.
We count a preference only when both orders select the same semantic response; any disagreement, including preference versus tie, is recorded as a tie.
For a contrast written as ``A vs.\ B'', we report wins/losses/ties (W/L/T) for A and compute its win rate over non-tied order-consistent comparisons as $W/(W+L)$.

The primary generator and evaluator come from the same model family, a deviation from the pre-specified plan for a different-family judge, and related models can show preference leakage or self-preference~\citep{li2026preference,panickssery2024selfpreference}.
We therefore rescore selected system comparisons with \modelCross\ as a separate descriptive robustness check rather than averaging results from the two evaluators.

\paragraph{Pre-specified statistical testing.}
We fixed the confirmatory comparisons before scoring to avoid choosing favorable tests after observing results.
For each benchmark and generation run, an exact two-sided sign test compares the non-tied prompt-level wins and losses for every pre-specified contrast~\citep{dixonmood1946}.
Testing several related contrasts increases the chance of at least one false positive, so we apply Holm's step-down correction~\citep{holm1979} across each benchmark's 24 pre-specified tests, eight contrasts in each of three generation runs.
Generation runs remain separate because they repeat the same prompts rather than provide new independent samples.\footnote{See Appendix~\ref{app:pairwise-detail} for the generation-run estimates, Wilson intervals~\citep{wilson1927}, and corrected test decisions.}

To summarize each contrast across generation runs, we compute a post-hoc prompt-level estimate that complements the pre-specified tests.
For each prompt, win, tie, and loss outcomes are mapped to $1$, $1/2$, and $0$, averaged across the three generation runs, and summarized per benchmark and across benchmarks with a bootstrap interval~\citep{efron1979bootstrap} and an exact sign test over prompts.

\paragraph{Robustness and behavioral analysis.}
Pairwise LLM evaluation can favor longer outputs and can change with judge identity~\citep{zheng2023judging,dubois2024length,saito2023verbosity,li2026preference,panickssery2024selfpreference}.
We therefore test whether the pairwise direction persists within pre-specified output-length bands, at equal length under a logistic model on the log length ratio~\citep{dubois2024length}, under a compute match, and under the cross-family evaluator.
We analyze observable traces of process transitions, goal creation and development, goal replacement, and subagent invocations to characterize how the loop is used.
