We design the experiments to answer three progressively narrower questions: whether process-level organization improves document quality, which parts of \condAgenticCogWriter\ account for any advantage, and how the resulting writing loop behaves in practice. The study contains seven pre-specified systems plus one post-hoc execution check, all compared on the same task inputs and under a common generation contract.

\subsection{Systems compared}
\label{sec:baselines}

\paragraph{Alternative writing architectures.}
We compare \condAgenticCogWriter\ with three ways of organizing long-form generation. The one-shot baseline (\condSinglePass) asks the model to produce the complete document in one call. The fixed-stage baseline (\condStaged) executes \texttt{Pre-Write}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Write}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Re-Write}, following the common pattern of separating planning, drafting, and revision into a prescribed workflow~\citep{yang2022re3,wan2025cognitive}. The adaptive task-planning baseline (\condTaskPlanning) follows a WriteHERE-style task graph~\citep{xiong2025beyond}: it recursively decomposes the assignment and selects among dependency-satisfied task nodes. Thus, the three alternatives respectively advance the whole document, a fixed writing stage, or an adaptive task node, whereas \condAgenticCogWriter\ advances a writing process.

\paragraph{Ablations of \condAgenticCogWriter.}
We next modify one architectural choice at a time while leaving the rest of the process loop intact. \condNoGoals\ removes the explicit goal network but retains adaptive process selection and the specialized agents. \condFixedOrder\ keeps the shared draft, goal network, process history, and specialists but replaces the \texttt{Monitor}'s adaptive decision with the repeated \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle. These ablations test whether explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process order are individually necessary for the full system's performance.

\paragraph{Execution variants.}
We also probe whether the way writing processes are executed can explain the result. \condSingleWriter\ retains the goal network and process-level loop but performs planning, drafting, and reviewing in one writer context without role-agent delegation; it also limits each \texttt{Translating} step to one paragraph before the next process decision. Because those changes are bundled, this condition is diagnostic rather than a clean ablation. After observing the main results, we added \condSingleContext\ as a narrower exploratory check: it preserves the full process loop and shared state but executes each selected process in the \texttt{Monitor}'s context rather than in a separate role-agent context. The run also differs in gateway entry point and transport, so we use it only to constrain, rather than establish, a role-context explanation. \subsection{Benchmarks and prompts}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

To evaluate whether the architecture transfers across different forms of long-form writing, we use three complementary benchmarks. WritingBench~\citep{wu2026writingbench} covers broad real-world writing requests and supplies task-specific evaluation criteria. HelloBench~\citep{que2024hellobench} contains long-text tasks across categories such as summarization, open-ended generation, and text completion, with checklist-based evaluation. DoLoMiTes~\citep{malaviya2025dolomites} focuses on methodical expert writing in which the output must follow explicit objectives, procedures, inputs, and constraints. Together, the three benchmarks probe general instruction following, sustained long-form generation, and structured expert-document production rather than a single writing genre.

Because our goal is to stress architectural differences beyond routine short-form fluency, we pre-commit to a demanding slice in which architecture has more opportunity to matter. For each benchmark, the selector excludes pilot prompts and ranks the remaining examples by prompt word count plus the largest explicitly requested output length, when one is stated; the top 100 eligible prompts form the \texttt{hard100} slice, for 300 prompts total. What makes this slice demanding differs across benchmarks: WritingBench combines long inputs with explicit long-output requirements, HelloBench is dominated by very long-input summarization and continuation tasks and rarely states an output length, and DoLoMiTes emphasizes methodical expert constraints with more moderate prompt length. The selector is therefore an operational stress test for sustained generation and context handling, not an independent semantic measure of writing difficulty. Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} gives the deterministic selection rule and benchmark-specific composition.

The 100-prompt count was fixed before scoring. Under the observed missing and tie rates, its inferential sensitivity is nevertheless substantial: a benchmark cell contains about \PowerCellDecided\ decided comparisons, for which an exact sign test at the strictest first-step Holm threshold has 80\% power for a true preference of about \PowerCellDetectablePreference. Pooling benchmarks only to characterize scale gives about \PowerPooledDecided\ decided comparisons and a corresponding detectable preference of about \PowerPooledDetectablePreference. These calculations describe the sensitivity of the pre-committed sample rather than making the \texttt{hard100} slice representative of each full benchmark; the full pools were not judged.

\subsection{Generation setup}
\label{sec:implementation}

All generations use \modelGen\ inside the same Codex-style coding-agent harness. We chose Luna for generation because this study requires high-volume long-form generation across systems, prompts, and replications, and OpenAI describes it as a model optimized for cost-sensitive, high-volume workloads and as the fastest, lowest-cost member of the GPT-5.6 family~\citep{openai2026luna,openai2026gpt56}. Holding that generator fixed across conditions makes the comparison about writing architecture rather than base-model capability.

\condAgenticCogWriter\ is packaged as a skill whose \texttt{Monitor} can invoke \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, and \texttt{Reviewer} through the harness's subagent mechanism; the comparison systems use the same harness and task-level information policy. The implementation follows the platforms' documented skill and subagent abstractions~\citep{anthropicAgentSkills,anthropicSubagents,openaiBuildSkills,openaiSubagents,googleAntigravitySkills,googleAntigravitySubagents}. Each prompt--system pair is generated in three independent replications. The harness records completed outputs and, where applicable, process switches, goal events, delegated invocations, token accounting, and timing. Network access, web search, and external retrieval are disabled for every condition so that architecture cannot change the information available to the writer. Appendix~\ref{app:runtime} reports the complete runtime settings.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\paragraph{Pointwise evaluation.}
We first score each completed document independently with \modelJudge. We use Sol because it is the GPT-5.6 flagship for complex professional work and is reported to outperform Luna across multiple professional and reasoning evaluations, giving the study a higher-capability evaluator than generator~\citep{openai2026sol,openai2026gpt56}. WritingBench retains its five query-specific criteria, each scored from 1 to 10, while HelloBench retains its 5--7 item benchmark checklist and five-level item scale; the native score is the mean of the corresponding criterion or checklist scores. DoLoMiTes does not provide a corresponding native score in our setup. We preserve the benchmark evaluation prompts but substitute \modelJudge\ for the judge models used in the original benchmark reports.\footnote{The native scores therefore describe our shared-generator implementations under the benchmark rubrics and should not be compared numerically with published leaderboard values.}

We additionally apply a common five-dimension rubric covering instruction fulfillment, organization and coherence, content adequacy and depth, style and audience fit, and factual or constraint fidelity. The judge assigns each dimension a raw score from 1 to 5. Because a ten-prompt pilot showed that these raw scores clustered near the top of the scale, we standardize each dimension within a benchmark across outputs from all seven pre-specified conditions and then average the five z-scores. The resulting composite is explicitly relative: zero is the across-condition mean for that benchmark, and a negative value means below that reference mean rather than poor absolute writing quality. The pointwise and benchmark-native scores come from the initial replication and are descriptive. Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} reproduces the evaluator prompts and benchmark-specific procedures.

\paragraph{Pairwise evaluation.}
The pilot also showed that direct comparison separated systems more clearly than the compressed pointwise scale, so we ask \modelJudge\ to compare two responses written for the same prompt and return a winner or tie. LLM judges are known to exhibit position bias~\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair}; to reduce that bias, every pair is evaluated twice, once as A-versus-B and once with the responses swapped to B-versus-A. We retain a prompt-level winner only when both orders identify the same semantic winner, and record any disagreement, including winner versus tie, as a tie. Prompts lacking a completed output from either system are excluded from that contrast.

The Luna--Sol assignment deliberately gives judging to the higher-capability model, but both models remain in the same GPT-5.6 family. That does not satisfy our separately pre-specified plan to use a different-family judge. Related generator--judge models, including members of the same model family, can introduce systematic preference leakage or self-preference~\citep{li2026preference,panickssery2024selfpreference}. We therefore disclose the deviation and repeat the selected architecture contrasts with \modelCross\ as a descriptive cross-family robustness check rather than averaging the two judges.

\paragraph{Pre-specified statistical testing.}
To avoid choosing favorable contrasts or tests after seeing the results, we fixed the inferential family before scoring. The eight contrasts compare \condAgenticCogWriter\ with \condSinglePass, \condStaged, \condTaskPlanning, \condNoGoals, and \condFixedOrder, and compare \condSingleWriter\ with \condSinglePass, \condAgenticCogWriter, and \condNoGoals. Within each benchmark, each contrast is tested separately in each replication with an exact two-sided sign test over non-tied prompt-level wins and losses~\citep{dixonmood1946}; Holm correction~\citep{holm1979} is then applied jointly to the resulting pre-specified cells for that benchmark. We never pool prompts across replications for confirmatory inference. Main-text tables report descriptive replicate means, while Appendix~\ref{app:pairwise-detail} reports replication-level Wilson intervals~\citep{wilson1927} and the multiplicity-adjusted decisions.

\paragraph{Robustness and behavioral analysis.}
Two known properties of automatic judging motivate the sensitivity analyses. LLM evaluators can prefer longer responses~\citep{zheng2023judging,dubois2024length}, while related generator--judge models can introduce preference leakage~\citep{li2026preference,panickssery2024selfpreference}. We therefore check whether the pairwise direction persists within pre-specified output-length bands, under a tight compute match, and under the cross-family judge; because output length and compute are consequences of execution, the first two checks are descriptive rather than causal adjustments.

Aggregate quality scores also cannot show how an adaptive writing agent uses its control loop. We therefore analyze observable traces of process transitions, goal creation and refinement, goal replacement, and delegated invocations. We also compare outcomes from runs that complete one planning--drafting--reviewing cycle with outcomes from runs that take another process path; this trace-defined comparison is observational, not randomized.
