We treated the cognitive process theory of writing as a set of executable hypotheses about how a writing agent should be controlled. A controller that acts over writing processes is preferred to single-pass, staged, and adaptive task-planning organizations on the demanding long-form prompts tested here. The mechanism results are more selective: removing explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive process ordering produces no detectable loss under our confirmatory design, and the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} mostly converges to a simple canonical cycle. Collapsing the process roles into one writer produces a substantial loss, but the current intervention does not separate role or context separation from recursion granularity and output length. The result therefore supports choosing among writing processes as a useful way to control long-form generation while leaving the source of its remaining advantage as the next mechanism to isolate.

More broadly, these experiments show a way to use cognitive theory in language-agent research: not only as architectural inspiration, but as a source of commitments that can be implemented, intervened on, and rejected when they fail to explain behavior.
