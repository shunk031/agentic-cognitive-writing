We made the cognitive process theory of writing executable on a coding agent and tested it. On the most demanding prompts of three benchmarks, the writer that delegates \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} to separate agents wins most comparisons against single-pass, staged, and task-planning writers, and its margin over single-pass writing survives output-length matching and a change of judge family. Of the theory's three commitments, only the split into separate agents made a measurable difference: removing the goal network or the dynamic process order showed no detectable loss, and the \texttt{Monitor} mostly followed the textbook order on its own. The ablations cannot separate that split from its side effects on output length and granularity, and the confirmatory judgments come from the generator's own model family, so the result is a bounded one. For writers who use coding agents, our evidence supports delegating the three processes and shows no detectable gain from goal bookkeeping or from letting the \texttt{Monitor} reorder them; for the theory, its control dynamics did not show up in the product.
