We used the cognitive process theory of writing to pose testable hypotheses about language-agent control rather than as a blueprint to import wholesale. On product-level diagnostics, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is competitive but not uniformly best; in the matched-prompt primary evaluation, it is preferred to \condSinglePass, \condStaged, and \condTaskPlanning\ on the long-form slices tested here. These comparisons provide evidence of an organization-level performance difference, not the causal effect of the process-level action space alone. Within the process-organized design, removing the explicit goal representation or replacing adaptive process ordering with a fixed cycle produces no detectable loss. The unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows that cycle, and realized departures from it do not track a larger pairwise advantage. The bundled \condSingleWriter\ result and narrower exploratory \condSingleContext\ check likewise do not isolate a single execution mechanism.

The broader takeaway is that cognitive theory can be most informative for language-agent research when its commitments are made executable and tested separately. Here, the confirmatory interventions do not identify explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive ordering as sources of the observed advantage, while the exploratory evidence does not support a simple role-context explanation. Features still shared by the successful process-organized implementations, such as explicit process decomposition and repeated returns through the \texttt{Monitor}, remain targets for follow-up intervention rather than mechanisms established here. \condAgenticCogWriter\ therefore serves less as a cognitive architecture to adopt wholesale than as a vehicle for turning theory into falsifiable agent-design hypotheses.
