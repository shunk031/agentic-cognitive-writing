We used the cognitive process theory of writing to pose testable hypotheses about language-agent control rather than as a blueprint to import wholesale. On benchmark-level product diagnostics, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is competitive but not uniformly best across every metric; in the matched-prompt primary evaluation, it is preferred to \condSinglePass, \condStaged, and \condTaskPlanning\ on the long-form slices tested here. These comparisons establish a performance difference between the tested organizations, not the causal effect of the process-level action space alone. Within the process-organized design, removing the explicit goal representation or replacing adaptive process ordering with a fixed cycle produces no detectable loss under our confirmatory design. The unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} itself usually follows that cycle, and a descriptive trace--outcome split does not show a larger preference gap when its realized sequence departs from the canonical cycle. The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ comparison shows a large gap but bundles several execution changes, while a narrower post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check that retains the process loop shows no detectable difference after pooling benchmark outcomes within its exploratory replication.

The broader takeaway is that cognitive theory can be most informative for language-agent research when its commitments are made executable and tested separately. Here, the confirmatory interventions do not identify explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive ordering as sources of the observed advantage, and the exploratory evidence does not support separated role contexts as its explanation. The remaining question is narrower: which features shared by the successful process-organized implementations, such as explicit process decomposition and repeated returns through the \texttt{Monitor}, account for the difference? Those features are not isolated by the present experiments. \condAgenticCogWriter\ therefore serves less as a cognitive architecture to adopt wholesale than as a vehicle for turning a theory into falsifiable design hypotheses.
