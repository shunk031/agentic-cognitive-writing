We use the cognitive process theory of writing to pose testable hypotheses about language-agent control rather than to import a cognitive architecture wholesale. Agentic CogWriter is preferred to single-pass generation, fixed writing stages, and adaptive task planning on the tested long-form benchmark slices, yet the confirmatory interventions do not identify explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering as sources of that advantage. The unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} itself usually follows the fixed process cycle, and a post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check does not show a detectable pooled loss when separated role contexts are removed while the process loop is retained.

The broader lesson is methodological: cognitive theory can be most informative for language-agent research when its commitments are made executable and tested separately. Here, the results narrow the remaining question to features shared by the successful process-organized conditions, such as process decomposition and repeated returns through the \texttt{Monitor}, without establishing which of them is causal. Agentic CogWriter therefore serves as a vehicle for falsifiable design hypotheses rather than an architecture to adopt wholesale.
