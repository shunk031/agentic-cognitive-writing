We used the cognitive process theory of writing to pose testable hypotheses about language-agent control rather than as a blueprint to import wholesale. On benchmark-level product diagnostics, Agentic CogWriter is competitive but not uniformly best across every metric; in the matched-prompt primary evaluation, it is preferred to single-pass generation, fixed writing stages, and adaptive task planning on the long-form slices tested here. Yet removing the explicit goal representation or replacing adaptive process ordering with a fixed cycle produces no detectable loss under our confirmatory design, and the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} itself usually follows that cycle. The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ comparison shows a large gap but bundles several execution changes, while a narrower post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check does not show a detectable pooled loss when separate role-agent contexts are removed and the process loop is retained.

The broader takeaway is that cognitive theory can be most informative for language-agent research when its commitments are made executable and tested separately. Here, the confirmatory interventions do not identify explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive ordering as sources of the observed advantage, and the exploratory evidence weakens separated role contexts as its explanation. The remaining question is narrower: which features shared by the successful process-organized implementations, such as explicit process decomposition and repeated returns through the \texttt{Monitor}, account for the difference? Those features are not isolated by the present experiments. Agentic CogWriter therefore serves less as a cognitive architecture to adopt wholesale than as a vehicle for turning a theory into falsifiable design hypotheses.
