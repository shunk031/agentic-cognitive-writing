\paragraph{Cognitive models of writing.}
Cognitive accounts of writing treat composition as coordinated activity rather than a linear sequence of stages. \citet{flower1981cognitive} describe \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} as recurrent processes coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor} and guided by writer-generated goals. Later work distinguishes knowledge-telling from knowledge-transforming~\cite{bereiter1987psychology}, models text production as knowledge generation~\cite{galbraith1999writing}, and revises the original architecture~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}. We use the 1981 account because it makes process control explicit enough to operationalize, not because we treat a language-agent implementation as a model of human cognition.

\paragraph{Structured long-form generation.}
Language-model writing systems already introduce substantial structure into generation. Plan-and-Write and Re$^3$ plan before realization~\cite{yao2019plan,yang2022re3}; Self-Refine iterates feedback and revision~\cite{madaan2023self}; STORM strengthens pre-writing and outlining~\cite{shao2024assisting}; and LongWriter's AgentWrite decomposes long outputs into manageable subtasks~\cite{bai2025longwriter}. More adaptive systems interleave decomposition and execution: WriteHERE recursively expands and executes a task graph~\cite{xiong2025beyond}, while IS-CoT cycles through planning, writing, and reflection~\cite{sun2026cot}. These systems differ in scheduling and feedback, but their evolving control units are generally plans, sections, passages, or task nodes. Our question is whether the controller's action space should instead consist of writing processes.

\paragraph{Process-oriented and cognitively inspired writing systems.}
Writing-process theory has also informed systems for human--AI co-writing and writing support~\cite{gero2022design,wan2024coco,siddiqui2025script,zhou2026iris}. CogWriter~\cite{wan2025cognitive} is the closest autonomous system: it combines hierarchical planning, generation, monitoring, and reviewing under a cognitive-writing motivation. Our distinction is therefore not that cognition has never inspired a writing system. We instead make the \emph{choice of writing process} the controller's explicit action and treat the resulting process-selection policy as an object of intervention: explicit goals can be removed, adaptive ordering can be fixed, and process roles can be collapsed. Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism-comparison} compares these mechanisms with representative systems. This framing also connects to language-agent work on explicit control loops and role-structured computation~\cite{sumers2024cognitive,yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion}.
