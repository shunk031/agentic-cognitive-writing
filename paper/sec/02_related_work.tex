\paragraph{Cognitive models of writing.}
Writing research offers several accounts of how composition is organized. Flower and Hayes's cognitive process theory~\cite{flower1981cognitive} describes \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} as recurrent processes coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}, with writer-generated goals that can change during composition. Later accounts distinguish knowledge-telling from knowledge-transforming~\cite{bereiter1987psychology}, treat writing as knowledge generation~\cite{galbraith1999writing}, and revise the original cognitive architecture~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}. We use the 1981 account because its three named processes, coordinating \texttt{Monitor}, and mutable goals can be translated into explicit choices and records in a writing agent. This is an engineering operationalization rather than a claim of cognitive equivalence.

\paragraph{Structured long-form generation.}
Language-model writing systems already externalize substantial structure beyond token-level generation. Plan-and-Write and Re$^3$ organize realization around plans~\cite{yao2019plan,yang2022re3}; STORM drafts sections from an outline~\cite{shao2024assisting}; and LongWriter's AgentWrite decomposes a long response into subtasks~\cite{bai2025longwriter}. WriteHERE goes further by recursively expanding a task graph and adaptively scheduling retrieval, reasoning, and composition nodes~\cite{xiong2025beyond}. Other systems organize generation through repeated process-like stages: Self-Refine alternates generation, feedback, and refinement~\cite{madaan2023self}, while IS-CoT embeds a Plan--Write--Reflect cycle into generation~\cite{sun2026cot}. The distinction relevant here is not whether a system plans or revises, but what its controller can choose next. Existing systems generally advance a content item or task, or execute a prescribed process loop. Agentic CogWriter instead makes the writing processes themselves the controller's adaptive action space.

\paragraph{Cognitively inspired writing systems.}
Writing-process theory has also informed systems for human--AI co-writing and writing support~\cite{gero2022design,wan2024coco,siddiqui2025script,zhou2026iris}. CogWriter~\cite{wan2025cognitive} is the closest autonomous system: it uses hierarchical planning, parallel generation agents, and monitoring and reviewing mechanisms that trigger revision. CogWriter therefore already establishes cognitive writing theory as an architectural motivation for long-form generation. Our question is narrower: once \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} become the actions available to a \texttt{Monitor}, which commitments of that control formulation actually matter? We test explicit goals, adaptive process ordering, and separated process execution under a common generator and coding-agent harness. Appendix~\ref{app:decision-comparison} gives a descriptive comparison with representative systems.
