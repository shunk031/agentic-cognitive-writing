\paragraph{Writing as a controlled process.}
Research on writing has long treated composition as more than a linear progression through fixed stages. Flower and Hayes~\cite{flower1981cognitive} recast writing as recurrent coordination among processes, while later theories emphasize knowledge transformation, knowledge generation, and revised accounts of cognition and affect in writing~\cite{bereiter1987psychology,galbraith1999writing,hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}. For our purposes, the important feature of this literature is that it exposes separable claims about control: what operations are available during composition, what state guides them, and how their ordering can change as writing proceeds. We use the 1981 model selectively because its named writing processes, coordinating \texttt{Monitor}, and mutable goals can be operationalized as explicit agent actions and records. This use treats cognitive theory as a source of testable design commitments rather than as a claim that a language agent reproduces human cognition.

\paragraph{Structured long-form generation.}
Long-form language-model systems differ in what advances generation. Plan-and-Write and Re$^3$ organize generation around plans and planned passages~\cite{yao2019plan,yang2022re3}; STORM advances an outline section by section~\cite{shao2024assisting}; and LongWriter's AgentWrite decomposes a requested document into subtasks before producing the corresponding parts~\cite{bai2025longwriter}. WriteHERE makes the next-step decision adaptive by recursively expanding a heterogeneous task graph and scheduling dependency-satisfied retrieval, reasoning, and composition nodes~\cite{xiong2025beyond}. Other systems organize generation through recurring process-like stages rather than content units: Self-Refine alternates generation, feedback, and refinement~\cite{madaan2023self}, while IS-CoT interleaves Plan--Write--Reflect during generation~\cite{sun2026cot}. These systems differ in many respects and were not designed to isolate a common control variable. The axis relevant to our study cuts across them: whether the controller advances content or task units, follows a prescribed process sequence, or can choose among writing processes themselves. Appendix~\ref{app:decision-comparison} summarizes representative systems using this descriptive vocabulary.

\paragraph{Cognitively inspired writing systems.}
Cognitive writing models have also informed interactive writing-support systems and analyses of human--AI co-writing~\cite{gero2022design,wan2024coco,siddiqui2025script,zhou2026iris}. These works use cognitive processes to organize where AI assistance enters writing, how contributions are categorized, or how writers can inspect and revise their work. CogWriter~\cite{wan2025cognitive} is the closest autonomous long-form system: it combines hierarchical planning, parallel generation agents, monitoring, and review in an architecture explicitly motivated by cognitive writing theory. Our contribution is therefore not to introduce cognitive theory as an inspiration for writing agents. Instead, we turn a narrower set of control commitments into experimentally separable variables: the writing processes are the \texttt{Monitor}'s action space, the goal representation can be removed, process ordering can be fixed, and execution contexts can be altered. This lets us ask not only whether a cognitively inspired organization works, but which of its executable commitments affect outcomes and how the resulting controller actually behaves.
