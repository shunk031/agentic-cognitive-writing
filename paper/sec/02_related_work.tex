\paragraph{Writing as a controlled process.}
Writing research has long treated composition as more than a fixed sequence of stages. Flower and Hayes~\cite{flower1981cognitive} describe writing as recurrent coordination among processes, while later theories emphasize knowledge transformation, knowledge generation, and revised accounts of cognition and affect~\cite{bereiter1987psychology,galbraith1999writing,hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}. This literature separates questions about control that are often bundled in agent implementations: which operations are available, what state guides them, and how their ordering can change during composition. We use the 1981 model selectively because its named processes, coordinating \texttt{Monitor}, and mutable goals can be represented as explicit agent operations and records. We therefore treat the theory as a source of testable design commitments, not as a claim of cognitive equivalence.

\paragraph{Structured long-form generation.}
Long-form language-model systems differ in what advances generation. Plan-and-Write and Re$^3$ organize generation around plans and planned passages~\cite{yao2019plan,yang2022re3}; STORM advances an outline section by section~\cite{shao2024assisting}; and LongWriter's AgentWrite decomposes a document into subtasks~\cite{bai2025longwriter}. WriteHERE makes next-step choice adaptive by recursively expanding a heterogeneous task graph and scheduling dependency-satisfied retrieval, reasoning, and composition nodes~\cite{xiong2025beyond}. Other systems use recurring process-like stages: Self-Refine alternates generation, feedback, and refinement~\cite{madaan2023self}, while IS-CoT interleaves Plan--Write--Reflect~\cite{sun2026cot}. The comparison relevant here is therefore not whether a system is structured, but what its controller advances: a content or task unit, a prescribed process sequence, or a dynamically selected writing process. This distinction motivates our controlled baselines: a fixed staged workflow tests structured execution without adaptive process choice, while adaptive task planning tests whether any advantage is explained by adaptive decomposition over task nodes. Appendix~\ref{app:decision-comparison} summarizes representative systems using this descriptive vocabulary.

\paragraph{Cognitively inspired writing systems.}
Cognitive writing models have also informed interactive writing support and analyses of human--AI co-writing~\cite{gero2022design,wan2024coco,siddiqui2025script,zhou2026iris}. CogWriter~\cite{wan2025cognitive} is the closest autonomous long-form system, combining hierarchical planning, parallel generation agents, monitoring, and review under cognitive-writing motivation. Our contribution is therefore not to introduce cognitive theory as inspiration for writing agents. Instead, we use the theory to define a process-level control organization and then separate commitments that are otherwise bundled: the explicit goal representation can be removed, process ordering can be fixed, and execution contexts can be altered. This lets us test both whether process-organized control differs from alternative organizations and which executable commitments account for that difference.
