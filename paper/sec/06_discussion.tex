Tables~\ref{tab:main-results} and~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} show that \condAgenticCogWriter's clearest advantage is consistent preference in direct same-prompt comparisons rather than uniform pointwise dominance.
Our system has the highest displayed means on WritingBench and HelloBench, while \condStaged\ has the highest DoLoMiTes pointwise mean without a paired interval separating it from our system.
Pairwise evaluation favors our system over single-pass generation, fixed writing stages, and adaptive task planning on every benchmark, supporting process-level control as an organizational advantage rather than a guarantee of maximizing every metric.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} supports a system-level interpretation.
Removing explicit goals or fixing process order leaves performance near the full system, whereas the larger \condSingleWriter\ gap points to execution structure; the post-hoc \condSingleContext\ diagnostic does not reduce that gap to delegation alone.
The evidence therefore favors the coordinated writing loop over any single tested mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} and Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} show that adaptivity is selective: \condAgenticCogWriter\ usually follows planning--drafting--reviewing and departs only occasionally.
Process-level control should therefore not be equated with continual replanning.
Relative to systems that adapt task decomposition or structural reasoning during generation~\citep{xiong2025beyond,wan2025cognitive,sun2026cot}, our design adapts which writing action is selected.

Current long-input tasks may be demanding without requiring process changes; benchmarks that change objectives, introduce evidence, or impose rhetorical constraints during composition could test process control more directly.
More broadly, cognitive writing theory offers executable control abstractions by making alternative writing processes explicit enough to implement, compare, and falsify.
