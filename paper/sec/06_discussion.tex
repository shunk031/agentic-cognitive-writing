Direct same-prompt comparisons provide the strongest evidence for \condAgenticCogWriter. The system is preferred to one-shot generation, a fixed-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning, whereas pointwise scoring is more mixed. The result therefore supports process-organized writing as a useful system design under the tested evaluations without implying universal superiority across benchmarks or metrics.

The experiments do not isolate which part of that design produces the advantage. Removing explicit goals or forcing a planning--drafting--reviewing order yields no reliable difference in the pre-specified tests, and the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows that same cycle. The remaining explanation may lie in properties shared by the successful process-organized conditions---persistent state, process decomposition, repeated returns to the \texttt{Monitor}, or delegated execution---but the present ablations do not identify a single cause.

The evaluated tasks also leave limited opportunity to test genuinely adaptive writing behavior. Long inputs, long requested outputs, and procedural constraints make document production demanding, but they do not necessarily require replanning after drafting begins. Future evaluations should stress process control with tasks whose objectives, evidence, or rhetorical constraints change during composition. Better writing control could then benefit not only standalone writing systems but also autonomous research workflows whose final value depends on the report or manuscript they produce~\citep{schmidgall2025agentlab,lu2024aiscientist}. Cognitive writing theory provides testable design abstractions for that agenda rather than a claim of psychological fidelity.
