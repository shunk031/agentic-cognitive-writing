Tables~\ref{tab:main-results} and~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ is distinguished by consistent same-prompt preference rather than uniform pointwise dominance, supporting process-level control without requiring every benchmark-specific metric to peak.
Because the ablations keep most of that advantage, it resides in the process-level loop the variants share; explicit goals and adaptive ordering appear partly redundant on tasks that seldom require revision.

Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} and Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ usually follows planning--drafting--reviewing and departs selectively, so process-level control is not the same as continual replanning.
This complements systems that adapt task decomposition or structural reasoning during generation~\citep{xiong2025beyond,wan2025cognitive,sun2026cot}: a second axis is which process a controller selects, not only how dynamically it replans.

The process findings motivate benchmarks that directly induce revision: long inputs and, where stated, long requested outputs, together with procedural constraints, can be demanding without requiring earlier decisions to be revisited.
Such benchmarks could change objectives, introduce new evidence, or impose rhetorical constraints; more broadly, cognitive writing theory provides executable control abstractions rather than claims that language agents reproduce human cognition, making alternative writing processes explicit enough to implement, compare, and falsify.
