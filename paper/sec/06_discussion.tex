\subsection{What does the performance pattern establish?}

The product-level and pairwise results answer different questions. On the benchmark-native and pointwise diagnostics, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is strongest among the controlled conditions on WritingBench and HelloBench but not uniformly best on DoLoMiTes. These scores show that the process-organized condition is competitive on the selected long-form slices, not that it dominates every benchmark metric or published system. The matched-prompt pairwise evaluation provides the primary RQ1 evidence: under same-family judging, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to \condSinglePass, \condStaged, and \condTaskPlanning. The two views are not interchangeable estimands: the pointwise composite summarizes independently scored product criteria from the initial replication, whereas the pairwise analysis directly compares matched outputs within each replication and then summarizes the replicate-specific estimates descriptively. Taken together, the results establish a performance difference between the tested organizations while keeping the scope of that claim tied to our controlled generator, harness, prompt slice, and evaluation protocol. They do not by themselves identify the process-level action space as the causal source of that difference.

\subsection{What explains the remaining advantage?}

The ablations narrow that performance result without identifying a single mechanism. The confirmatory interventions do not identify either the explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering as the source of the advantage. The process traces sharpen these two results differently. The unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows the simple \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle, which directly contextualizes why imposing that order may change little in these runs. Moreover, splitting \condAgenticCogWriter\ runs by whether they follow exactly that cycle does not reveal a larger preference gap for non-canonical sequences against either \condFixedOrder\ or \condSingleWriter. Observable departures from the canonical schedule therefore do not track the pairwise advantage in these descriptive analyses. Accepted goal regeneration is also rare, but this speaks only to replacement of existing goals; it does not show that the goal representation is inactive, because goals are also created and developed during execution. The traces help characterize the interventions without explaining the causal effect of removing the goal representation or scheduling flexibility.

The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ result is informative mainly by contrast with the narrower \condSingleContext\ check. \condSingleWriter\ performs much worse, but it bundles role-context separation, delegation, how much text is produced between process decisions, and output length. \condSingleContext\ removes separated role contexts while retaining the process loop and shows no detectable difference after pooling benchmark outcomes within its exploratory replication, but that run also differs in gateway entry point and transport. It is therefore narrower than \condSingleWriter\ without being a clean context-only intervention. Because \condSingleContext\ is post-hoc exploratory evidence rather than a confirmatory equivalence test, these comparisons constrain a simple role-context explanation without establishing an alternative mechanism.

Other simple explanations are also insufficient on their own. Delegation count does not track preference: \condTaskPlanning\ spawns more delegated work than \condAgenticCogWriter\ yet loses the pairwise comparison. Greater realized inference expenditure likewise does not straightforwardly account for the pattern because the \condAgenticCogWriter\ advantage over \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning\ persists within the tested compute-matching band. Output length remains a concern, especially for \condSingleWriter, but the length-matched \condAgenticCogWriter--\condSinglePass\ analysis shows that length alone does not explain every main contrast. These analyses constrain possible explanations; they are not causal mediation estimates.

What remains shared across the successful process-organized conditions includes explicit decomposition into writing processes, repeated returns to the \texttt{Monitor}, multi-step execution, and other implementation details. The current experiments do not isolate these features from one another.

\subsection{Implications for writing-agent design}

The experiments separate how writing is decomposed from how the resulting work is scheduled. Under the tested design, removing explicit goal bookkeeping or constraining adaptive process ordering produces no detectable loss, and the exploratory same-context result does not provide evidence that the benefit depends on assigning each process to a separate role-agent context. One remaining hypothesis is that organizing multi-step computation around explicit writing processes and repeatedly returning through a common controller matters more than dynamic scheduling, persistent goal bookkeeping, or role-context separation. Because those shared features are not isolated from one another, this is a target for follow-up intervention rather than a mechanism established here.

\subsection{Implications for theory-driven agent design}

The cognitive process theory is useful here less as a blueprint to reproduce literally than as a source of executable commitments. Its named processes provide a computational decomposition, while its claims about mutable goals and flexible ordering become design choices that can be intervened on separately. The negative and null results are therefore part of the contribution: they narrow which theory-derived commitments plausibly account for the observed gain under the current design. This is an engineering result about language-agent organization, not evidence that the corresponding cognitive mechanisms are unnecessary for human writing.
