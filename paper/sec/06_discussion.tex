The clearest result is a system-level advantage under direct same-prompt comparison. \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to one-shot generation, the fixed-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning, while pointwise scoring is more mixed and does not place the system first on every benchmark. The evidence therefore supports process-organized writing as a useful design for a general-purpose agent, not a claim that the system dominates every alternative under every metric.

The mechanism evidence is substantially weaker than the system-level result. Removing the explicit goal representation or forcing a planning--drafting--reviewing order produces no reliable difference in the pre-specified multiple-comparison-corrected tests, and the exploratory prompt-level analysis suggests only a small benefit from adaptive ordering. The traces offer a compatible explanation: the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows the same planning--drafting--reviewing cycle and rarely replaces an existing goal. Explicit goals and adaptive ordering may still matter on particular tasks, but neither component explains the much larger gaps to the alternative writing systems in the present evaluation.

The evaluated prompts also limit what the process traces can establish. Our deterministic subsets emphasize long inputs, long requested outputs, or methodical constraints, but those properties do not guarantee that a task requires repeated replanning. A stronger stress test for adaptive control would introduce writing problems whose objectives, evidence, or rhetorical constraints change during composition. The execution diagnostics likewise leave several coupled explanations unresolved: \condSingleWriter\ changes multiple properties at once, while the narrower \condSingleContext\ check weakens a simple subagent-context explanation without isolating a replacement mechanism.

The broader implication is that writing can be treated as a stateful process inside a general-purpose agent rather than as a final generation call. Coding-agent-style harnesses already provide persistent files, skills, and subagent execution; organizing those capabilities around writing processes creates a testable interface between agent control and document production. That interface can also matter in larger autonomous research workflows whose final artifact is a report or manuscript~\citep{schmidgall2025agentlab,lu2024aiscientist}. Cognitive writing theory provides the design vocabulary for our implementation, but the traces describe agent behavior rather than human cognition, and the ablations test engineering choices rather than psychological fidelity.
