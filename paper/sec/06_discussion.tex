\subsection{What explains the remaining advantage?}

The central empirical pattern separates the performance of process-organized execution from the incremental value of several theory-derived commitments. Agentic CogWriter is preferred to the three baseline organizations, yet the confirmatory interventions do not identify either the explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering as the source of that advantage. The process traces point in the same direction: the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows the simple \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle and rarely regenerates goals. The post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check further weakens an attribution to separated role contexts because collapsing the three processes into one agent context does not produce a detectable pooled loss while the process loop is retained. What remains shared across the successful process-organized conditions includes explicit decomposition into writing processes, repeated returns to the \texttt{Monitor}, multi-step execution, and other implementation details. The current experiments do not isolate these features from one another.

The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ result is therefore informative mainly by contrast with \condSingleContext. \condSingleWriter\ performs much worse, but it bundles role-context separation, delegation, how much text is produced between process decisions, and output length. \condSingleContext\ changes role-context separation more narrowly and does not show the same pooled loss, but it is post-hoc exploratory evidence rather than a confirmatory equivalence test. Together, these comparisons rule out a simple reading of the single-writer gap as evidence for separate agent contexts while leaving the shared process-loop structure as an open target for follow-up intervention.

Two other simple explanations are insufficient on their own. More delegation does not guarantee better writing: \condTaskPlanning\ spawns more delegated work than Agentic CogWriter yet loses the pairwise comparison. Greater realized inference expenditure also does not straightforwardly account for the pattern, because the Agentic CogWriter advantage over \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning\ persists within the tested compute-matching band. Output length remains a concern, especially for the single-writer comparison, but the length-matched Agentic CogWriter--\condSinglePass\ analysis shows that length alone does not explain every main contrast. These are constraints on possible explanations, not causal mediation estimates.

\subsection{Implications for writing-agent design}

The experiments separate two questions that are often coupled in structured generation: how to decompose writing and how to schedule the resulting work. Under the tested design, Agentic CogWriter is preferred to the baseline organizations, while explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process ordering add no detectable benefit over their corresponding interventions. The exploratory same-context result also suggests that the benefit need not depend on assigning each process to a separate role-agent context. A simpler candidate explanation is therefore that organizing multi-step computation around explicit writing processes and returning through a common controller may matter more than dynamic scheduling or persistent goal bookkeeping. Because those shared features are not isolated from one another, this remains a hypothesis for follow-up experiments rather than a mechanism established here.

\subsection{Implications for theory-driven agent design}

The cognitive process theory is useful here less as a blueprint to reproduce literally than as a source of executable commitments. Its named processes provide a computational decomposition, while its claims about mutable goals and flexible ordering become design choices that can be intervened on separately. In our experiments, the observed advantage survives confirmatory interventions on two commitments, and a post-hoc exploratory check does not support separated role contexts as the missing explanation. The negative and null results are therefore part of the contribution: they narrow which theory-derived commitments plausibly account for the observed gain under the current design. This is an engineering result about language-agent organization, not evidence that the corresponding cognitive mechanisms are unnecessary for human writing.
