\subsection{What explains the remaining advantage?}

The results leave a specific mechanistic gap. Agentic CogWriter is preferred to the three baseline organizations, yet the two single-factor interventions do not identify either the explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering as the source of that advantage. The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ contrast points to the remaining implementation differences, but it bundles separate process contexts, delegation, how much text is produced between process decisions, and output length. The next mechanism experiment should therefore separate these factors rather than treating the single-writer comparison as evidence for any one of them.

Two observations further constrain this explanation without resolving it. First, more delegation is not sufficient by itself: \condTaskPlanning\ spawns more delegated work than Agentic CogWriter yet loses the pairwise comparison. Second, greater realized inference expenditure does not straightforwardly account for the pattern, because the Agentic CogWriter advantage over \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning\ persists within the tested compute-matching band. These observations narrow the explanation space, but they do not distinguish process-specific context separation from delegation, decision granularity, or output length.

\subsection{Implications for writing-agent design}

The experiments separate process decomposition from dynamic process scheduling. Under the tested design, organizing generation around distinct writing processes is more successful than the three baseline organizations, while making the process order adaptive and maintaining an explicit goal network do not yield detectable gains over their corresponding interventions. This suggests that a useful design question is not only how flexibly a controller schedules work, but also how the writing operations themselves are instantiated and separated. Because the intervention results do not establish equivalence, simpler process schedules should be treated as a hypothesis for follow-up rather than as a general replacement for adaptive control.

\subsection{Implications for theory-driven agent design}

The cognitive process theory is useful here less as a blueprint to reproduce literally than as a source of executable commitments. Its named processes define a computational decomposition, while its claims about mutable goals and flexible ordering become design choices that can be intervened on directly. In our experiments, the process decomposition remains associated with the strongest system, whereas the current evidence does not identify mutable goals or adaptive ordering as necessary mechanisms. This is an engineering result about language-agent organization, not evidence that the corresponding cognitive mechanisms are unnecessary for human writing.
