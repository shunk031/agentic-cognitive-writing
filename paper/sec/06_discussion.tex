Tables~\ref{tab:main-results} and~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ is distinguished by consistent same-prompt preference rather than uniform pointwise dominance, supporting process-level control without requiring every benchmark-specific metric to peak.
Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} further suggests a system-level interpretation: removing goals or fixing process order leaves performance near the full system, whereas the larger \condSingleWriter\ gap points to execution structure; the post-hoc \condSingleContext\ diagnostic does not isolate that gap to subagent delegation.
The evidence therefore supports the coordinated writing loop more strongly than any single tested mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} and Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ usually follows planning--drafting--reviewing and departs selectively, so process-level control should not be equated with continual replanning.
This complements systems that adapt task decomposition or structural reasoning during generation~\citep{xiong2025beyond,wan2025cognitive,sun2026cot}: another design axis is what writing action a controller selects, not only how dynamically it replans.

These process findings motivate benchmarks that directly induce revision: long inputs and, where stated, long requested outputs, together with procedural constraints, can be demanding without requiring earlier decisions to be revisited.
Future benchmarks could change objectives, introduce new evidence, or impose rhetorical constraints during composition.
More broadly, cognitive writing theory provides executable control abstractions rather than claims that language agents reproduce human cognition, making alternative writing processes explicit enough to implement, compare, and falsify.
