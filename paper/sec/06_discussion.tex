\subsection{What the comparison supports}

The two evaluation views answer different questions. Pointwise and benchmark-native scores assess each generated document in isolation and show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ is competitive but not uniformly strongest. Matched-prompt pairwise evaluation, by contrast, consistently prefers \condAgenticCogWriter\ to the three alternative control organizations tested here. Taken together, the evidence supports an organization-level difference under our generator, harness, prompt slice, and evaluation protocol. It does not by itself show that dynamically selecting among writing processes is the unique causal source of that difference.

\subsection{What the interventions rule out}

The single-factor interventions narrow the explanation. Removing the explicit goal network does not significantly worsen pairwise preference, so the observed advantage cannot be attributed simply to maintaining that record. Forcing the canonical planning--drafting--reviewing order also does not significantly worsen preference. The execution traces make this second result less surprising: even when free to choose, the \texttt{Monitor} usually follows that order, and the less common sequences are not associated with a larger pairwise advantage.

The execution diagnostics also resist a simple explanation in terms of separate role contexts. \condSingleWriter\ performs substantially worse, but that intervention changes delegation, control granularity, and output length at the same time. The narrower \condSingleContext\ run keeps the process loop and shows no significant pooled difference in one exploratory replication, but it also changes gateway entry point and transport. Length- and compute-matched analyses preserve important pairwise directions, yet they condition on realized execution outcomes and are not causal mediation tests. The current evidence therefore narrows several candidate explanations without identifying one isolated mechanism.

\subsection{Implications for agentic writing systems}

What remains shared by the successful process-organized variants is the broader execution pattern: explicit decomposition into writing activities, persistent state across those activities, and repeated returns to a common decision point before the document is complete. These features are natural targets for future interventions. More broadly, the study illustrates a useful role for cognitive writing theory in agent design. Rather than treating the theory as an architecture to copy literally, we use it to expose system choices that can be implemented, ablated, and inspected. The resulting negative findings are informative: they separate the success of the overall agentic organization from specific components that might otherwise be assumed to explain it.
