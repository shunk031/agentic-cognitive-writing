Tables~\ref{tab:main-results} and~\ref{tab:pairwise-results} show that the clearest advantage of \condAgenticCogWriter\ is not uniform dominance under every pointwise score, but consistent preference in direct same-prompt comparisons.
The pattern supports process-level control as a useful way to organize long-form generation without requiring it to maximize every benchmark-specific metric.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-results} suggests that the advantage is better interpreted at the system level than assigned to either explicit goals or adaptive ordering alone.
Near-parity results for \condNoGoals\ and \condFixedOrder\ and the larger \condSingleWriter\ gap point to execution structure as another candidate explanation, but the post-hoc \condSingleContext\ diagnostic does not reduce that gap to subagent delegation alone.
The current evidence therefore supports the coordinated writing loop more strongly than any single tested mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:process-sequences} and Table~\ref{tab:process-dynamics} show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ usually follows planning--drafting--reviewing and departs from that path only selectively, so process-level control should not be equated with continual replanning.
This perspective complements systems that adapt task decomposition or structural reasoning during generation~\citep{xiong2025beyond,wan2025cognitive,sun2026cot}: another design axis is not only how dynamically a writer replans, but what kind of writing action its controller selects.

The process findings suggest a more targeted evaluation agenda for writing agents.
Long inputs and, where stated, long requested outputs, together with procedural constraints, make document production demanding but do not necessarily create reasons to revisit earlier decisions.
Future benchmarks could test process control directly by changing objectives, introducing new evidence, or imposing rhetorical constraints during composition.
More broadly, cognitive writing theory provides executable control abstractions rather than claiming that language agents reproduce human cognition: it makes alternative writing processes explicit enough to implement, compare, and falsify.
