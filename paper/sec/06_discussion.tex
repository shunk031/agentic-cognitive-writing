\subsection{What explains the remaining advantage?}

The central empirical pattern separates process-organized execution from the incremental value of several design commitments. Agentic CogWriter is preferred to the three baseline organizations, yet the confirmatory interventions do not identify explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering as the source of that advantage. Consistently, the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows the simple \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle and rarely regenerates goals. The post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check also produces no detectable pooled loss when separated role contexts are removed but the process loop is retained. What remains shared across the successful process-organized conditions includes explicit decomposition into writing processes, repeated returns to the \texttt{Monitor}, multi-step execution, and other implementation details that the present experiments do not isolate.

The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ result should therefore not be read as evidence for any one of those features. It bundles role-context separation, delegation, how much text is produced between process decisions, and output length, whereas \condSingleContext\ changes role-context separation more narrowly and does not show the same pooled loss. Because \condSingleContext\ is post-hoc exploratory evidence rather than a confirmatory equivalence test, the comparison constrains a simple role-context explanation without establishing an alternative mechanism.

Two other simple explanations are insufficient on their own. Delegation count does not track preference: \condTaskPlanning\ spawns more delegated work than Agentic CogWriter yet loses the pairwise comparison. The advantage over \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning\ also persists within the tested compute-matching band. Output length remains a concern, especially for \condSingleWriter, but the length-matched Agentic CogWriter--\condSinglePass\ analysis shows that length alone does not explain every main contrast. These are constraints on possible explanations, not causal mediation estimates.

\subsection{Implications for writing-agent design}

The experiments separate how writing is decomposed from how the resulting work is scheduled. Under the tested design, explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process ordering add no detectable benefit over their corresponding interventions, and the exploratory same-context result provides no evidence that the benefit depends on separate role-agent contexts. One remaining hypothesis is that organizing multi-step computation around explicit writing processes and repeatedly returning through a common controller matters more than dynamic scheduling or persistent goal bookkeeping. Because those shared features are not isolated from one another, this is a target for follow-up intervention rather than a mechanism established here.

\subsection{Implications for theory-driven agent design}

The cognitive process theory is useful here less as a blueprint than as a source of executable commitments. Its named processes provide a computational decomposition, while mutable goals and flexible ordering become choices that can be intervened on separately. The negative and null results are therefore part of the contribution: they narrow which theory-derived commitments plausibly account for the observed gain under the current design. This is an engineering result about language-agent organization, not evidence that corresponding cognitive mechanisms are unnecessary for human writing.
