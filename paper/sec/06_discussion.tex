\subsection{What does the performance pattern establish?}

The product-level diagnostics and matched-prompt pairwise evaluation answer different questions: the former situate output quality on benchmark-oriented metrics, while the latter directly compares alternative organizations under a shared prompt and serves as the primary RQ1 outcome. Together they provide evidence that the tested process-organized system differs in performance from the baseline organizations under our generator, harness, prompt slice, and evaluation protocol. They do not identify the process-level action space itself as the causal source of that difference.

\subsection{What explains the remaining advantage?}

The confirmatory interventions do not identify either the explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering as the source of the advantage. The traces make the scheduling result easier to interpret: the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows the canonical \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle, and departures from that cycle do not track a larger pairwise advantage in the descriptive trace--outcome split. Accepted goal regeneration is also rare, but this speaks only to replacement of existing goals; goals are still created and developed, so the traces do not show that the explicit goal representation is inactive.

The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ result is informative mainly by contrast with the narrower \condSingleContext\ check. \condSingleWriter\ bundles role-context separation, delegation, control granularity, and output length, whereas \condSingleContext\ retains the process loop and shows no detectable across-benchmark difference within its exploratory replication. Because the latter also differs in gateway entry point and transport and is not a confirmatory equivalence test, the two comparisons constrain a simple role-context explanation without identifying an alternative mechanism.

Other simple explanations are also insufficient on their own. Delegation count does not track preference: \condTaskPlanning\ spawns more delegated work than \condAgenticCogWriter\ yet loses the pairwise comparison. Greater realized inference expenditure likewise does not straightforwardly account for the pattern because the \condAgenticCogWriter\ advantage over \condStaged\ and \condTaskPlanning\ persists within the tested compute-matching band. Output length remains a concern, especially for \condSingleWriter, but the length-matched \condAgenticCogWriter--\condSinglePass\ analysis shows that length alone does not explain every main contrast. These analyses constrain possible explanations; they are not causal mediation estimates.

What remains shared across the successful process-organized conditions includes explicit decomposition into writing processes, repeated returns to the \texttt{Monitor}, multi-step execution, and other implementation details. The current experiments do not isolate these features from one another.

\subsection{Implications for writing-agent design}

The experiments separate how writing is decomposed from how the resulting work is scheduled. Under the tested design, removing explicit goal bookkeeping or constraining adaptive process ordering produces no detectable loss, and the exploratory same-context result does not support a simple dependence on separate role-agent contexts. One remaining hypothesis is that organizing multi-step computation around explicit writing processes and repeatedly returning through a common controller matters more than dynamic scheduling, persistent goal bookkeeping, or role-context separation. Because those shared features are not isolated from one another, this is a target for follow-up intervention rather than a mechanism established here.

\subsection{Implications for theory-driven agent design}

The cognitive process theory is useful here less as a blueprint to reproduce literally than as a source of executable commitments. Its named processes provide a computational decomposition, while its claims about mutable goals and flexible ordering become design choices that can be intervened on separately. The negative and null results are therefore part of the contribution: they narrow which theory-derived commitments plausibly account for the observed gain under the current design. This is an engineering result about language-agent organization, not evidence that the corresponding cognitive mechanisms are unnecessary for human writing.
