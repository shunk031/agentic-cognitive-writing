Long-form writing is a control problem as much as a generation problem. Producing a coherent document requires repeatedly deciding whether to develop content, turn intentions into prose, inspect what has been written, or revise its direction. Language-model writing systems already externalize parts of this control through planning, decomposition, feedback, and revision~\cite{yang2022re3,madaan2023self,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond}. Yet their controllers typically choose plans, passages, sections, or task nodes rather than the writing processes that produce them.

In the cognitive process theory of writing~\cite{flower1981cognitive}, \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} recur in no fixed order and are coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}; writer-generated goals can themselves change as composition proceeds. Later accounts revise and extend this model~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the 1981 account is especially useful computationally because it makes explicit commitments about how writing is controlled. We use those commitments not as a claim that a language agent reproduces human cognition, but as executable hypotheses about the organization of writing computation. This leads to two questions: does a language agent improve when its controller chooses among writing processes, and, if so, which commitments of that controller account for the improvement?

We turn process-level control into an executable language-agent architecture, Agentic CogWriter. Its \texttt{Monitor} observes the current draft, an explicit representation of writer-generated goals called the \emph{goal network}, and the history of prior process choices, then selects \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, or \texttt{Reviewing}. A corresponding role agent executes the selected process before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. This architecture exposes three commitments that can be tested separately: maintaining an explicit evolving goal representation, choosing the process order adaptively rather than following a fixed cycle, and executing the three writing processes in distinct role contexts. Figure~\ref{fig:agentic-cogwriter} contrasts choosing the next writing process with choosing the next section, passage, or task, and summarizes the interventions used to test these commitments.

We evaluate these control strategies on demanding long-form writing tasks drawn from three complementary benchmarks: WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench} for general-purpose writing, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench} for diverse long-text generation, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites} for structured expert writing. Within each benchmark, a pre-specified rule selects the longest-output slice, where architectural differences have the most room to affect composition. Under the same generator and harness, we compare process-level control with single-call generation, a fixed three-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning across independent replications.

We make three contributions. First, we formulate long-form generation as \emph{process-level control}: the controller's action space consists of writing processes, so it chooses whether to plan, translate, or review next rather than which section, passage, or task to work on. Second, we operationalize three theory-derived commitments as architectural interventions: explicit goals, adaptive process ordering, and separated process roles. Third, we show that the full process-level architecture outperforms the tested control organizations even though removing explicit goals or adaptive ordering produces no detectable loss under our confirmatory design. This leaves the source of the remaining advantage as an open mechanistic question.
