Long-form writing is a control problem as much as a generation problem. Producing a coherent document requires repeatedly deciding whether to develop content, turn intentions into prose, inspect what has been written, or revise its direction. Language-model writing systems already externalize parts of this control through planning, decomposition, feedback, and revision~\cite{yang2022re3,madaan2023self,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond}. Yet their controllers typically act over units of work---plans, passages, sections, or task nodes---rather than over the writing processes that produce those units.

In the cognitive process theory of writing~\cite{flower1981cognitive}, \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} recur in no fixed order and are coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}; writer-generated goals can themselves change as composition proceeds. Later accounts revise and extend this model~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the 1981 account is especially useful computationally because it makes explicit commitments about how writing is controlled. We use those commitments not as a claim that a language agent reproduces human cognition, but as executable hypotheses about the organization of writing computation. This leads to two questions: does a language agent improve when its controller chooses among writing processes, and, if so, which commitments of that controller account for the improvement?

We turn process-level control into an executable language-agent architecture, Agentic CogWriter. Its \texttt{Monitor} observes the current draft, an explicit representation of writer-generated goals---the \emph{goal network}---and the history of prior process choices, then selects \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, or \texttt{Reviewing}. A corresponding role agent executes the selected process before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. This architecture exposes three commitments that can be tested separately: maintaining an explicit evolving goal representation, choosing the process order adaptively rather than following a fixed cycle, and executing the three writing processes in distinct role contexts. Figure~\ref{fig:agentic-cogwriter} contrasts this process-level control with task-level control and summarizes the interventions used to test these commitments.

To make differences in control observable, we evaluate on demanding long-form tasks rather than routine short-form generation. Using a pre-specified length-based rule, we select the 100 prompts requesting the longest outputs from each of three complementary benchmarks: WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench} for general-purpose writing, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench} for diverse long-text generation, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites} for structured expert writing. This yields 300 prompts spanning different kinds of extended composition. Under the same generator and harness, we compare process-level control with single-call generation, a fixed three-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning, and repeat every comparison across three independent replications.

Process-level control improves model-judged writing against all three organizations: it is preferred in \PooledFourOneRateMean\ of non-tied comparisons against single-call generation, \PooledFourTwoRateMean\ against fixed stages, and \PooledFourThreeRateMean\ against adaptive task planning. The mechanism evidence is more selective. Removing the explicit goal representation or fixing the process order produces no Holm-surviving benchmark cell. Consistent with this, the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} follows the canonical process cycle in \AfourCycleCompliantRate\ of completed runs and accepts goal regeneration in only \AfourRegenerationRuns\ of \AfourRunCount\ runs. Collapsing the process roles into one writer produces a substantial loss, but that intervention also changes recursion granularity and output length, so it does not by itself identify role separation as the cause.

Our contribution is therefore not simply a cognitively inspired writing agent. First, we formulate long-form generation as \emph{process-level control}, in which the controller chooses what kind of writing operation to execute next rather than which section, passage, or task to execute. Second, we turn commitments from a cognitive theory into explicit architectural interventions under a common generator and harness, allowing product outcomes to be interpreted together with process traces. Third, the experiments localize the observed benefit away from explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process scheduling while leaving the remaining mechanism deliberately unresolved. The evidence is scoped to the tested setting: generation uses one model family, and the primary evaluation relies on a judge from the same family.
