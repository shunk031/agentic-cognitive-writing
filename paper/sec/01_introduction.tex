Large language model agents increasingly combine reasoning, tool use, memory, and delegation to carry out multi-step knowledge work~\citep{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion,sumers2024cognitive}. Writing is becoming part of that broader workflow: intelligent writing assistants support planning and revision~\citep{gero2022design}, while autonomous research systems culminate in reports or scientific manuscripts~\citep{schmidgall2025agentlab,lu2024aiscientist}. These settings make long-form writing more than a final decoding step. An agent must decide how to organize a document, turn partial plans into prose, inspect what has been written, and revise when the document no longer serves the task.

Existing long-form generation systems address this coordination problem by imposing structure on generation~\citep{yao2019plan,yang2022re3,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond,sun2026cot}. Some build an outline before drafting, some generate section by section, some recursively decompose a document into tasks, and others interleave structural reasoning with text generation. These approaches have made long-form generation substantially more reliable, but they usually define progress in terms of \emph{content units} such as sections, passages, or task nodes, or in terms of a prescribed sequence of stages. A different architectural question remains comparatively underexplored: \emph{should an agent decide which kind of writing process is needed next?}

The cognitive process theory of writing~\citep{flower1981cognitive,hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling} provides a useful abstraction for this question. \citet{flower1981cognitive} characterize writing as the coordination of \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing}, with a \texttt{Monitor} deciding which process to invoke as the document develops. The theory therefore suggests an agent architecture in which the unit of control is not the next section or task node, but the next writing process.

Building on \citet{flower1981cognitive}, we propose \condAgenticCogWriter, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:agentic-cogwriter}. It is a long-form writing architecture implemented on a coding-agent-style harness with persistent state and subagent delegation. A top-level \texttt{Monitor} observes the evolving draft, goals, and process history; chooses whether the next activity should be planning, drafting, or reviewing; delegates that activity to a specialized role agent; and then receives the updated state. The resulting writing trajectory is determined online rather than fixed in advance, which is the sense in which the system is \emph{agentic}.

We evaluate \condAgenticCogWriter\ on three complementary kinds of long-form writing. WritingBench~\citep{wu2026writingbench} probes broad, instruction-driven writing with task-specific criteria; HelloBench~\citep{que2024hellobench} stresses sustained long-text generation across heterogeneous task types; and DoLoMiTes~\citep{malaviya2025dolomites} targets methodical expert documents with explicit procedures and constraints. To focus on cases where architecture has room to matter, we use a pre-committed 300-prompt slice selected for long inputs and, where prompts specify them, long requested outputs, and compare all systems under the same generator and information policy. Independent scoring and direct same-prompt comparisons show that \condAgenticCogWriter\ is competitive on all three benchmarks and is preferred to one-shot generation, a fixed writing pipeline, and adaptive task planning.

Beyond asking whether the architecture works, we examine \emph{how a writing agent actually uses the freedom it is given}. We ablate the explicit goal network and adaptive process ordering, vary whether writing processes execute in separate role contexts, and analyze the resulting process traces. The resulting picture is more informative than a single benchmark win: neither the goal network nor flexible ordering alone explains the performance difference, and the supposedly flexible \texttt{Monitor} usually realizes a simple planning--drafting--reviewing cycle. This behavior analysis turns the system from a black-box writing pipeline into an experimentally inspectable agent architecture.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate long-form writing for general-purpose agents as a process-level architecture problem and introduce \condAgenticCogWriter, a concrete realization on a coding-agent harness. Second, we compare that architecture with one-shot, fixed-stage, and adaptive task-planning organizations across complementary long-form benchmarks using replicated direct comparisons and pre-specified statistical tests. Third, we use ablations and execution traces to identify what the observed advantage does \emph{not} depend on, providing empirical constraints for future writing-agent design rather than attributing the result to cognitive theory by analogy.
