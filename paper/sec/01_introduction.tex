Large language model agents increasingly combine reasoning, tool use, memory, and delegation to carry out multi-step knowledge work~\citep{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion,sumers2024cognitive}. Writing is becoming part of that broader workflow: intelligent writing assistants support planning and revision~\citep{gero2022design}, while autonomous research systems culminate in reports or scientific manuscripts~\citep{schmidgall2025agentlab,lu2024aiscientist}. Long-form writing inside an agent therefore requires more than a final decoding step: the agent must organize the document, turn plans into prose, inspect what has been written, and decide when revision is needed.

Existing long-form writing systems impose different forms of structure on generation~\citep{yao2019plan,yang2022re3,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond,sun2026cot}. Some build an outline before drafting~\citep{yao2019plan,yang2022re3}, some generate a document section by section~\citep{shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter}, some recursively decompose the document into tasks~\citep{xiong2025beyond}, and others interleave structural reasoning with text generation~\citep{sun2026cot}. Despite these differences, progress is usually defined by the next content unit, task node, or prescribed stage. A complementary design question remains comparatively underexplored: should the writer decide which kind of writing process is needed next?

Cognitive process theories of writing provide a natural abstraction for process-level control~\citep{flower1981cognitive,hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}. \citet{flower1981cognitive} characterize writing as coordination among \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing}, with a \texttt{Monitor} deciding which process to invoke as the document develops. Their formulation suggests a writing system whose control decision is not the next section or task node, but the next kind of writing work.

Our \condAgenticCogWriter\ is a long-form writing system implemented on a coding-agent-style harness with persistent state and subagent delegation. A top-level \texttt{Monitor} agent observes the evolving draft, goals, and process history, chooses whether the next activity should be planning, drafting, or reviewing, and delegates that activity to a corresponding subagent. The resulting process order is chosen during writing rather than fixed in advance.

We evaluate \condAgenticCogWriter\ on demanding subsets of three complementary long-form writing benchmarks spanning broad instruction-driven writing, long-context generation, and methodical expert-document production~\citep{wu2026writingbench,que2024hellobench,malaviya2025dolomites}. Every system uses the same generator and receives the same task information, allowing the comparison to focus on how writing is organized. Pointwise rubric scoring and direct same-prompt comparisons test document quality from complementary perspectives.

The component analyses ask whether the system-level result can be attributed to explicit goals or adaptive process order. Removing the goal representation or fixing the process order produces no reliable difference under the pre-specified multiple-comparison-corrected tests, while exploratory analysis suggests only a small advantage for adaptive ordering. Execution traces further show that the \texttt{Monitor} usually follows one planning--drafting--reviewing cycle, limiting how often adaptive ordering can matter.

Our contributions are threefold. (1) we formulate long-form writing for general-purpose agents as a process-level control problem and introduce a concrete system that chooses among planning, drafting, and reviewing during composition; (2) we compare this system with one-shot, fixed-stage, and adaptive task-planning alternatives across complementary long-form benchmarks; and (3) we use ablations and execution traces to test which proposed components explain the observed advantage and to characterize how the writing loop behaves in practice.
