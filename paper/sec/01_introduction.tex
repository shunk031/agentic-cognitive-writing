Long-form writing is a control problem as much as a generation problem. Producing a coherent document requires repeatedly deciding whether to develop content, turn intentions into prose, inspect what has been written, or revise its direction. Language-model writing systems already externalize parts of this control through planning, decomposition, feedback, and revision~\cite{yang2022re3,madaan2023self,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond}. We use \emph{controller} to refer to the part of a writing agent that decides what operation should happen next. In existing systems, that decision commonly concerns a plan element, passage, section, or task node rather than the writing process itself.

The cognitive process theory of writing~\cite{flower1981cognitive} offers a different unit of control. Flower and Hayes describe \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} as processes that can recur in no fixed order, coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}; they also propose that writer-generated goals can change as composition proceeds. Later accounts revise and extend this model~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the 1981 account is especially useful computationally because it makes explicit commitments about what is coordinated and what can change during writing. We treat those commitments as executable hypotheses about writing-agent control, not as a claim that a language agent reproduces human cognition.

We turn this idea into \emph{process-level control}, where the controller chooses which writing process to execute next. We instantiate it as Agentic CogWriter, a writing agent implemented on a coding-agent harness. Agentic CogWriter keeps three external records while writing: the current draft, an explicit hierarchical representation of current writing goals, and a history of previous process choices and goal changes. We call the goal representation the \emph{goal network}. The host agent implements the \texttt{Monitor}, which reads these records and chooses \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, or \texttt{Reviewing}. The corresponding \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, or \texttt{Reviewer} role agent executes that process before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. This design makes three theory-derived commitments directly testable: explicit goal representation, adaptive process ordering, and separated role contexts.

These commitments lead to two research questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether organizing generation around writing processes improves long-form writing relative to generation with no intermediate choice, a fixed stage sequence, and adaptive choice over task nodes. \textbf{RQ2} asks what controlled interventions and process traces reveal about the explicit goal representation, adaptive process ordering, and separated role contexts.

We evaluate Agentic CogWriter on long-form writing tasks drawn from three complementary benchmarks: WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench} for general-purpose writing, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench} for diverse long-text generation, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites} for structured expert writing. Within each benchmark, a pre-specified rule selects the longest-output slice, where differences between control strategies have more room to affect composition. Using the same generator and coding-agent harness, we compare Agentic CogWriter with single-call generation, a fixed three-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning across three independent replications. Agentic CogWriter is preferred to all three baselines, but the mechanism results are more selective: removing the explicit goal representation or replacing adaptive process ordering with a fixed \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle produces no detectable loss, and the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} itself usually follows that cycle. A diagnostic single-writer condition performs substantially worse, but it changes several execution factors together and therefore does not isolate separated role contexts.

We make three contributions. First, we turn commitments from cognitive writing theory into executable hypotheses that can be tested as interventions rather than imported wholesale as an architecture. Second, we formulate process-level control and instantiate it in Agentic CogWriter so that the unit of control, goal representation, process ordering, and role contexts are experimentally observable. Third, we show that the observed advantage over the tested baselines is not explained by detectable contributions from explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive process ordering under our design. This separates the value of writing-process decomposition from the stronger claim that current writing agents need the theory's dynamic scheduling commitments.
