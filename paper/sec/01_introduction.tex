Long-form writing is a control problem as much as a generation problem. Producing a coherent document requires repeatedly deciding whether to develop content, turn intentions into prose, inspect what has been written, or revise its direction. Language-model writing systems already externalize parts of this control through planning, decomposition, feedback, and revision~\cite{yang2022re3,madaan2023self,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond}. We use \emph{controller} to refer to the part of a writing agent that decides what operation should happen next. In existing systems, that decision commonly concerns a plan element, passage, section, or task node rather than the writing process itself.

The cognitive process theory of writing~\cite{flower1981cognitive} offers a different unit of control. Flower and Hayes describe \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} as processes that can recur in no fixed order, coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}; they also propose that writer-generated goals can change as composition proceeds. Later accounts revise and extend this model~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the 1981 account is especially useful computationally because it makes explicit commitments about what is coordinated and what can change during writing. We treat those commitments as executable hypotheses about writing-agent control, not as a claim that a language agent reproduces human cognition.

We turn this idea into \emph{process-level control}, where the controller chooses which writing process to execute next. We instantiate it as Agentic CogWriter, a writing agent implemented on a coding-agent harness. Agentic CogWriter keeps three external records while writing: the current draft, an explicit hierarchical representation of current writing goals, and a history of previous process choices and goal changes. We call the goal representation the \emph{goal network}. The host agent implements the \texttt{Monitor}, which reads these records and chooses \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, or \texttt{Reviewing}. The selected process is executed by the corresponding \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, or \texttt{Reviewer} subagent before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. This design exposes three commitments: maintaining an explicit evolving goal representation, choosing the process order adaptively rather than following a fixed cycle, and executing the three writing processes in separate \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, and \texttt{Reviewer} contexts.

These commitments lead to two research questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether organizing generation around writing processes improves long-form writing relative to generation with no intermediate choice, a fixed stage sequence, and adaptive choice over task nodes. \textbf{RQ2} asks what controlled interventions and process traces reveal about the three commitments above: the explicit goal representation, adaptive process ordering, and separate process-execution contexts.

We evaluate Agentic CogWriter on long-form writing tasks drawn from three complementary benchmarks: WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench} for general-purpose writing, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench} for diverse long-text generation, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites} for structured expert writing. Within each benchmark, a pre-specified rule selects the longest-output slice, where differences between control strategies have more room to affect composition. Using the same generator and coding-agent harness, we compare Agentic CogWriter with single-call generation, a fixed three-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning across independent replications.

We make three contributions. First, we formulate long-form generation as process-level control, in which the controller chooses among writing processes rather than sections, passages, or tasks. Second, we instantiate this formulation as Agentic CogWriter and turn three theory-derived commitments into experimentally observable design choices. Third, we show that Agentic CogWriter outperforms the tested baselines even though removing the explicit goal representation or adaptive process ordering produces no detectable loss under our confirmatory design. This leaves the source of the remaining advantage as an open mechanistic question.
