Large language models are increasingly used to produce long documents rather than isolated sentences or short responses. Recent systems support tasks such as story generation, Wikipedia-style article writing, constrained document generation, and technical reporting~\cite{yang2022re3,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,wan2025cognitive,xiong2025beyond}. In these settings, fluent local generation is not enough: a system must maintain document-level goals and structure, decide when additional planning is needed, turn plans into prose, inspect what has been written, and revise when the draft no longer serves the task. This challenge becomes more important as writing is embedded in broader agentic workflows, where producing a long document is itself a multi-step task rather than a single model call.

Existing long-form writing systems introduce substantial structure to address this problem. Some construct a plan or outline before drafting~\cite{yang2022re3,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter}; others alternate generation with feedback or reflection~\cite{madaan2023self,sun2026cot}; and recent writing agents adaptively decompose work into retrieval, reasoning, and composition tasks~\cite{xiong2025beyond}. These approaches improve long-horizon generation, but they usually organize control around a content unit, task node, or prescribed sequence of stages. Even when the workflow is adaptive, the system does not generally choose among the writing processes themselves as the next operation. This leaves a basic design question unresolved: how should an autonomous writing agent decide \emph{how to proceed} while a document is being written?

The cognitive process theory of writing offers a useful design principle for this problem. Flower and Hayes~\cite{flower1981cognitive} describe writing not as a fixed sequence of stages, but as recurrent coordination among planning, translating ideas into text, and reviewing, with a monitor deciding which process is needed as writing unfolds. They also emphasize that writer-generated goals can develop during composition. Later models revise and extend this account~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the original formulation suggests a concrete architecture for language agents: instead of fixing the next stage in advance or only choosing the next content task, an agent can repeatedly choose which writing process to perform next.

Inspired by Flower and Hayes, we propose \condAgenticCogWriter, an agentic long-form writing system built on a coding-agent harness. Figure~\ref{fig:agentic-cogwriter} gives an overview. A host agent acts as a \texttt{Monitor}: it observes a shared draft, an explicit record of writing goals, and the history of previous writing actions, then decides whether the document next needs planning, drafting, or reviewing. The selected process is delegated to a specialized \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, or \texttt{Reviewer} agent, whose output updates the shared workspace before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. The system is agentic in the operational sense that the next writing operation is selected from the current state at runtime and executed through repeated delegation, rather than being fully prescribed by a fixed end-to-end workflow.

We evaluate \condAgenticCogWriter\ on long-form tasks from WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench}, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench}, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites}. We first measure benchmark-native and standardized pointwise quality. \condAgenticCogWriter\ performs best among our controlled systems on WritingBench and HelloBench, while a staged writing workflow is strongest on the DoLoMiTes pointwise composite, so the product-level results do not show uniform dominance. We then compare matched outputs directly. In these pairwise comparisons, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to one-shot generation, a fixed pre-write--write--rewrite workflow, and an adaptive task-planning agent across the tested benchmarks and replications.

The experiments also probe which parts of the architecture matter. Removing the explicit goal record does not significantly worsen pairwise preference, and neither does replacing runtime process selection with a fixed planning--drafting--reviewing cycle under the pre-specified tests. Consistent with the latter result, the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} often follows that same cycle in its realized traces. Diagnostic and exploratory execution variants likewise do not isolate a single alternative mechanism. The evidence therefore supports the process-organized agent as a whole under our evaluation, while showing that its advantage cannot be attributed to explicit goal bookkeeping or adaptive process ordering alone.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate long-form writing-agent control around the choice of \emph{which writing process to execute next}, distinguishing this from advancing an outline section, task node, or fixed process stage.
    \item We propose \condAgenticCogWriter, a coding-agent-based architecture that realizes this idea through a state-aware \texttt{Monitor}, specialized writing agents, and persistent shared writing state.
    \item Through controlled comparisons, ablations, and execution traces, we show both where the process-organized design improves over alternative writing organizations and which seemingly central components do \emph{not} individually explain that improvement.
\end{itemize}
