Large language model agents~\citep{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion,sumers2024cognitive,luo2025llmagent} increasingly combine reasoning, tool use, memory, and delegation to carry out multi-step knowledge work. Recent coding agents have made this interactive, tool-mediated style of execution especially prominent~\citep{yang2024sweagent,wang2024openhands}. Beyond coding, agentic workflows increasingly support knowledge work whose outputs include reports or scientific manuscripts~\citep{schmidgall2025agentlab,lu2024aiscientist}. For agents that produce documents, long-form writing requires more than final text generation: the agent must organize the document, turn partial plans into prose, inspect what has been written, and decide when revision is needed.

Existing long-form writing systems impose different forms of structure on generation~\citep{yao2019plan,yang2022re3,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,wan2025cognitive,xiong2025beyond,sun2026cot}. Some build an outline before drafting~\citep{yao2019plan,yang2022re3}, some generate a document section by section~\citep{shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter}, some recursively decompose the document into tasks~\citep{xiong2025beyond}, and some adapt plans or structural reasoning during generation~\citep{wan2025cognitive,sun2026cot}. Taken together, these systems make document structure increasingly dynamic. We focus on a narrower control question that is not explicit in their formulations: should the writer choose among planning, drafting, and reviewing as the next writing process from a shared, evolving state?

The cognitive process theory of writing~\citep{flower1981cognitive,hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling} provides a useful abstraction for process-level control. \citet{flower1981cognitive} characterize writing as coordination among planning, translating, and reviewing, with a monitor deciding which process to invoke as the document develops. The resulting view suggests a writing system whose control decision is not the next section or task node, but the next kind of writing work.

Building on \citet{flower1981cognitive}, we propose \condAgenticCogWriter, a long-form writing system that implements process-level control on a coding-agent-style harness with persistent state and subagent delegation (Figure~\ref{fig:agentic-cogwriter}). A top-level Monitor agent observes the evolving draft, goals, and process history, chooses whether the next activity should be planning, drafting, or reviewing, and delegates that activity to a corresponding subagent. The resulting process order is chosen during writing rather than fixed in advance.

We evaluate our \condAgenticCogWriter\ on demanding subsets of three complementary long-form writing benchmarks spanning broad instruction-driven writing, long-context generation, and methodical expert-document production~\citep{wu2026writingbench,que2024hellobench,malaviya2025dolomites}. We hold the generator and task information constant across the compared conditions so that differences reflect how writing is organized. We assess document quality with both pointwise rubric scoring and pairwise same-prompt preference evaluation. Agentic CogWriter receives the strongest pointwise scores on WritingBench and HelloBench, while \condStaged\ is strongest on DoLoMiTes; in direct pairwise comparisons, Agentic CogWriter is preferred to one-shot generation, a fixed writing pipeline, and adaptive task planning. The component analyses remain close to the full system, supporting a system-level interpretation of the gain rather than attributing it to explicit goals or adaptive ordering alone. Process traces add a complementary result: most runs follow a simple planning--drafting--reviewing path, so adaptive ordering is available but used selectively.

Our contributions are threefold. (1) we formulate long-form writing for general-purpose agents as a process-level control problem and introduce a concrete system that chooses among planning, drafting, and reviewing during composition; (2) we compare our system with one-shot, fixed-stage, and adaptive task-planning alternatives across complementary long-form benchmarks; and (3) we use ablations and process analyses to test which components explain the observed advantage and characterize writing-loop behavior.
