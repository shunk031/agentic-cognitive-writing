Long-form writing requires decisions about both \emph{what} to write and \emph{how} to proceed. Language-model writing systems externalize these decisions through plans, outlines, task decomposition, feedback, and revision~\cite{yang2022re3,madaan2023self,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond}. We use \emph{controller} for the part of a writing agent that determines what operation happens next. Existing controllers commonly advance a plan element, passage, section, or task node, or execute a prescribed sequence of operations. They therefore usually select content or tasks rather than the writing process itself. This leaves a basic design question open: \emph{what should a writing agent be able to choose next?}

The cognitive process theory of writing~\cite{flower1981cognitive} suggests one answer. Flower and Hayes describe \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} as recurrent processes coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}, with writer-generated goals that can change during composition. Later accounts revise and extend this model~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}; we use the earlier formulation because it makes a process-level action space, flexible process ordering, and evolving goals explicit. We treat these commitments as experimentally separable hypotheses about agent control, not as a blueprint to copy wholesale or a claim of cognitive equivalence. Our question is which commitments matter once made executable.

We therefore formulate \emph{process-level control}: the controller chooses which writing process to execute next. Agentic CogWriter instantiates this formulation on a coding-agent harness. It maintains the current draft, an explicit hierarchical representation of current writing goals that we call the \emph{goal network}, and a history of process choices and goal changes. The host agent implements the \texttt{Monitor}, which reads these records and chooses \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, or \texttt{Reviewing}; the corresponding \texttt{Planner}, \texttt{Translator}, or \texttt{Reviewer} then executes that process before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. This makes explicit goal representation and adaptive process ordering directly testable, while separated role contexts remain an implementation choice that can be probed separately.

We ask three research questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether organizing generation around writing processes improves long-form writing relative to no intermediate choice, a fixed stage sequence, and adaptive choice over task nodes. \textbf{RQ2} asks what interventions reveal about explicit goal representation, adaptive process ordering, and separated role contexts. \textbf{RQ3} asks how the \texttt{Monitor} actually uses process-selection flexibility and goal changes.

We evaluate Agentic CogWriter on the longest-output slices of WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench}, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench}, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites}, using the same generator and coding-agent harness across conditions and independent replications. Agentic CogWriter is preferred to single-call generation, a fixed three-stage workflow, and adaptive task planning. Yet removing the explicit goal representation or fixing the \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} order produces no detectable loss, and the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} usually follows that cycle while rarely replacing goals. The diagnostic \condSingleWriter\ condition performs substantially worse but changes several execution factors together; a post-hoc \condSingleContext\ check that retains the process loop likewise shows no detectable pooled loss from removing separated role contexts. The tested interventions therefore do not identify a single mechanism that explains the baseline advantage.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we frame long-form writing-agent design around what the controller chooses next, distinguishing content or task selection from process-level control. Second, we turn theory-derived commitments into executable hypotheses that can be intervened on directly. Third, the experiments show a selective pattern: process-organized generation is preferred to the tested baseline organizations, while explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process ordering do not explain that advantage under the confirmatory design, and exploratory evidence does not support separated role contexts as its explanation. This illustrates how cognitive theory can serve as a source of falsifiable design hypotheses rather than architectural inspiration alone.
