AI agents are increasingly asked to carry out multi-step knowledge work that culminates in long-form artifacts. Autonomous research systems, for example, now include report or paper writing alongside literature review, coding, experimentation, and analysis~\cite{schmidgall2025agentlab,lu2024aiscientist}. This shift makes writing an architectural problem for agents, not only a text-generation problem for language models. Once an agent is responsible for producing a document over many steps, it must decide what to do between the initial instruction and the final text: when to plan, when to draft, when to inspect what has been written, and when to revise. We study how that writing loop should be organized in a general-purpose, coding-agent-style harness.

Existing long-form writing systems already add substantial structure beyond one-shot generation. Some build plans or outlines before drafting~\cite{yang2022re3,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter}; some alternate generation with feedback or reflection~\cite{madaan2023self,sun2026cot}; and recent writing agents dynamically decompose work into retrieval, reasoning, and composition tasks~\cite{xiong2025beyond}. These systems mainly structure \emph{what content or task is advanced next}, or prescribe a recurring sequence of stages. A different design choice is to let the agent decide \emph{what kind of writing work the document needs next}. That distinction matters in an agentic setting because the state of a long draft can change what operation is useful: more planning may be needed after drafting exposes a structural problem, while review may reveal that another drafting pass is preferable to further planning.

The cognitive process theory of writing provides a concrete starting point for this architectural question. Flower and Hayes~\cite{flower1981cognitive} characterize writing as recurrent coordination among planning, translating ideas into text, and reviewing, with a monitor determining which process to engage as composition unfolds. They also describe writer-generated goals as something that can develop during writing. Later theories revise and extend this account~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the original model is useful here because it separates three design decisions that an agent implementation can expose directly: which writing processes are available, what state guides process choice, and whether process order is fixed or selected online. We use the theory as a source of engineering hypotheses, not as a claim that language agents reproduce human cognition.

Inspired by this view, we propose \condAgenticCogWriter, a long-form writing architecture built on a coding-agent harness. A host agent maintains persistent writing state and repeatedly decides whether the document next needs planning, drafting, or reviewing. It delegates the selected activity to a specialized role agent, incorporates the resulting update into the shared workspace, and then decides again. The system is \emph{agentic} in the operational sense that its writing trajectory is determined online from the evolving document state rather than fully specified by a fixed workflow. This design turns a generic agent harness with a workspace and subagents into a writing system whose unit of control is the writing process itself.

We evaluate \condAgenticCogWriter\ on long-form tasks from WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench}, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench}, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites}. We first evaluate each output independently using benchmark-native scores and a standardized pointwise composite. \condAgenticCogWriter\ is strongest among our controlled systems on WritingBench and HelloBench, while a fixed staged workflow is strongest on the DoLoMiTes pointwise composite. We then compare matched outputs directly. In these pairwise comparisons, \condAgenticCogWriter\ is preferred to one-shot generation, a fixed pre-write--write--rewrite workflow, and adaptive task planning across the tested benchmarks and replications.

The component analyses make the result more selective. Removing the explicit goal record does not significantly worsen pairwise preference, and neither does replacing online process-order choice with a fixed planning--drafting--reviewing cycle. The execution traces help explain the latter result: when process order is left unrestricted, the host agent usually chooses that same cycle. Additional execution variants do not isolate a single alternative mechanism. The evidence therefore favors the process-organized agent as a whole under our evaluation, while leaving open which shared part of the architecture produces the advantage.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate long-form writing as an agent-architecture problem: beyond deciding what content to generate, an agent must decide what kind of writing operation to perform next.
    \item We propose \condAgenticCogWriter, a coding-agent-based architecture that combines persistent writing state, online process selection, and specialized planning, drafting, and reviewing agents.
    \item Through controlled architecture comparisons, component interventions, and execution traces, we show that the overall process-organized system is preferred under the primary pairwise evaluation, while explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process ordering do not individually explain that result.
\end{itemize}
