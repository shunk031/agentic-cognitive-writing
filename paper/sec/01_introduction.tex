Long-form writing is a control problem as much as a generation problem. Producing a coherent document requires repeatedly deciding whether to develop content, turn intentions into prose, inspect what has been written, or revise its direction. Language-model writing systems already externalize parts of this control through planning, decomposition, feedback, and revision~\cite{yang2022re3,madaan2023self,shao2024assisting,bai2025longwriter,xiong2025beyond}. Yet their controllers typically act over units of work---plans, passages, sections, or task nodes---rather than over the writing processes that produce those units.

Writing research offers a different abstraction. In the cognitive process theory of writing, \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, and \texttt{Reviewing} recur in no fixed order and are coordinated by a \texttt{Monitor}; writer-generated goals can themselves change as composition proceeds~\cite{flower1981cognitive}. Later accounts revise and extend this model~\cite{hayes1996new,hayes2012modeling}, but the 1981 account is especially useful computationally because it makes explicit commitments about how writing is controlled. We use those commitments not as a claim that a language agent reproduces human cognition, but as executable hypotheses about the organization of writing computation. This leads to two questions: does a language agent improve when its controller chooses among writing processes, and, if so, which commitments of that controller account for the improvement?

We operationalize this account as Agentic CogWriter. The \texttt{Monitor} observes the current draft, an explicit writer-goal network, and the history of prior process choices, then selects \texttt{Planning}, \texttt{Translating}, or \texttt{Reviewing}. The corresponding role agent executes that process before control returns to the \texttt{Monitor}. This formulation turns three commitments of the theory into interventions rather than design motivation alone: \condNoGoals\ removes the explicit goal network, \condFixedOrder\ replaces adaptive process choice with a fixed \texttt{Planning}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Translating}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Reviewing} cycle, and \condSingleWriter\ folds the process roles back into one writer. Figure~\ref{fig:agentic-cogwriter} contrasts this process-level action space with task-level control and summarizes the tested interventions.

We evaluate these organizations on the 100 prompts requesting the longest outputs from each of WritingBench~\cite{wu2026writingbench}, HelloBench~\cite{que2024hellobench}, and DoLoMiTes~\cite{malaviya2025dolomites}, with three independent replications. The process-level writer is preferred in \PooledFourOneRateMean\ of non-tied comparisons against \condSinglePass, \PooledFourTwoRateMean\ against \condStaged, and \PooledFourThreeRateMean\ against \condTaskPlanning. The mechanism results are more surprising. Removing the goal network or fixing the process order produces no Holm-surviving benchmark cell, while the unrestricted \texttt{Monitor} follows the canonical process cycle in \AfourCycleCompliantRate\ of completed runs. It returns from \texttt{Reviewing} to \texttt{Planning} in only \AfourReviewingToPlanningRate\ of runs, and accepted goal regeneration appears in only \AfourRegenerationRuns\ of \AfourRunCount\ runs. By contrast, \condSingleWriter\ wins only \PooledSevenFourRateMean\ of non-tied comparisons against the full system. This last contrast is suggestive rather than isolating: it also changes recursion granularity and output length, so it cannot by itself identify delegation as the cause.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it formulates long-form generation as \emph{process-level control}: the controller chooses which writing process to execute rather than which section, passage, or task to execute next. Second, it turns a cognitive theory's control commitments into explicit architectural interventions under a common generator and harness, allowing product outcomes to be read together with process traces. Third, the experiments localize the observed benefit away from the theory's more elaborate control dynamics: explicit goal bookkeeping and adaptive process ordering show little measurable contribution in this setting, while collapsing the process roles is associated with a substantial loss that warrants a cleaner test of process separation. The evidence is deliberately scoped to the tested setting: generation uses one model family, and the primary evaluation relies on a judge from the same family.
